## Part 1: The Problem and Baseline -- HAR (4 min)

> **[SLIDE 2: The Problem]**

### Why forecast realized volatility?

> **[SLIDE 2: The Problem -- stay here]**

"Realized variance -- the sum of squared intraday returns -- is the quantity that options ultimately pay off against. If you can forecast tomorrow's RV better than the market's implied estimate, you know when options are cheap and when they're expensive. That's where the edge lives.

Our universe is 21 symbols -- 17 mega-cap equities and 4 ETFs -- pooled together for training. 11 years of history: 2015 to May 2026, roughly 2,800 observations per symbol."

### HAR: the starting point

> **[SLIDE 3: HAR Baseline]**

"The Heterogeneous Autoregressive model -- Corsi 2009 -- is the standard baseline in the vol forecasting literature. It's beautifully simple: three features, one linear regression.

$$\log \widehat{RV}_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d \log RV_t + \beta_w \log \overline{RV}_{t-4:t} + \beta_m \log \overline{RV}_{t-21:t}$$

Where:
- $\log RV_t$ is today's log realized variance (daily component)
- $\log \overline{RV}_{t-4:t}$ is the 5-day rolling average (weekly component)
- $\log \overline{RV}_{t-21:t}$ is the 22-day rolling average (monthly component)

This captures the *heterogeneous market hypothesis*: different traders operate on different time scales. Day traders react to daily vol, portfolio managers to weekly, and pension funds to monthly. HAR's genius is decomposing volatility into these three cascading memory components."

### Training HAR: OLS vs Ridge vs Lasso

"I train HAR via OLS by default -- it's a 4-parameter model (3 slopes + intercept), so regularization isn't needed for HAR itself. But we also fit Ridge and Lasso variants:

- **OLS** -- the standard. Minimizes MSE in log-space. Unbiased but sensitive to collinearity between the three log-RV terms (which are structurally correlated -- weekly is a superset of daily, monthly is a superset of weekly).
- **Ridge** (L2) -- shrinks coefficients towards zero. In practice the regularization parameter is tiny for HAR because 4 parameters can't really overfit. But it stabilizes numerically in pooled training with 21 symbols.  
- **Lasso** (L1) -- does variable selection. For pure HAR this is uninteresting, but for the extended HARX models (HAR-IV, HAR-CJ) where we add implied vol or jumps, Lasso tells us which extensions actually help.

The critical extension is **HAR-IV**: adding the log of ATM implied volatility as a fourth regressor. This simple addition gives us forward-looking information -- IV embeds the market's expectation of future vol. HAR-IV dominates pure HAR by 100+ basis points in QLIKE at every horizon."

### Why log-space?

> **[SLIDE 4: HAR-IV & Log-Space]**

"All training is in log-variance space. RV is approximately log-normally distributed, so log-RV is approximately Gaussian -- this gives us well-behaved residuals, symmetric errors, and prevents negative variance forecasts by construction. The exponential retransformation uses Duan (1995) bias correction: $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \exp(\hat{y}) \cdot E[\exp(\epsilon)]$ where we estimate the smearing factor non-parametrically from in-sample residuals."

## Part 2: Residual Stacking -- HAR + LightGBM (4 min)

### The architecture

> **[SLIDE 5: Residual Stacking]**

"The champion model is not a pure LightGBM. It's a **residual stack**: HAR-IV provides the `init_score` -- a strong linear prior -- and LightGBM learns the *residuals* that the linear model can't capture.

Concretely:
1. Fit HAR-IV on the training fold → produces baseline predictions $\hat{y}_{base}$
2. Feed $\hat{y}_{base}$ as `init_score` to the LightGBM dataset  
3. LightGBM's gradient-boosted trees learn $f(X)$ where $y = \hat{y}_{base} + f(X)$
4. At prediction time: $\hat{y} = \hat{y}_{base}(X_{new}) + f_{tree}(X_{new})$

This is NOT the same as training LightGBM on raw residuals. The `init_score` mechanism means LightGBM's custom QLIKE objective function sees the *full* target but starts from the linear prediction. The gradients know about the base model -- it's end-to-end in loss space."

### Why not just LightGBM alone?

"Pure LightGBM without init_score starts from the global mean of log-RV. It has to learn the HAR relationship from scratch using tree splits -- which it can do, but wastes capacity. With init_score, the trees only need to learn what's *left over* after removing the linear structure. This gives:
- Faster convergence (fewer boosting rounds needed)
- Better OOS generalization (the linear component is stable; the trees handle nonlinearities)
- Interpretable decomposition: we can attribute QLIKE improvement to 'linear base' vs 'tree residual'"

### Tenor matching: the trial-036 insight

> **[SLIDE 6: Tenor Matching]**

"The key innovation of trial-036 is **per-horizon tenor matching** for the HAR-IV base:
- **h=1** (1-day ahead): use `har_iv_0dte` -- ATM IV from the 0-DTE expiry. This prices exactly tomorrow's realized vol with zero term premium contamination.
- **h=5** (5-day ahead): use `har_iv_1w` -- 1-week ATM IV. Exact match.
- **h=22** (22-day ahead): use `har_iv` -- 1-month ATM IV. Exact match.

Prior trials used 1-week IV for all horizons, contaminating h=1 with a 4-day term premium that HAR-IV was forced to estimate and subtract. Removing that contamination gained 8 bps at h=1."

## Part 4: SHAP Analysis and ALE (3 min)

> **[📊 TOGGLE DASHBOARD: Stay on SHAP Beeswarm tab. Point to the top features.]**

### How SHAP works

"SHAP -- SHapley Additive exPlanations -- assigns each feature a contribution to each individual prediction. For a prediction $\hat{y}_i$, SHAP satisfies:

$$\hat{y}_i = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_{ij}$$

where $\phi_0$ is the base value (mean prediction) and $\phi_{ij}$ is feature $j$'s contribution for observation $i$. The values come from game theory: each feature's marginal contribution averaged over all possible coalitions of other features.

For tree models, TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al. 2020) computes *exact* Shapley values in $O(TLD^2)$ time by exploiting the tree structure -- it traverses each tree and tracks the conditional expectation at each node. No approximation needed.

The beeswarm plot shows: x-axis is SHAP value (pushes prediction left/right), y-axis is feature rank, color is feature value (red=high, blue=low). So a red dot on the right means 'when this feature is high, it pushes the prediction up (higher vol forecast).'"

> **[📊 TOGGLE DASHBOARD: Click on the ALE tab for h=1]**

### How ALE works

"Accumulated Local Effects -- Apley & Zhu (2020) -- answers a different question: 'What is the *average* effect of feature $j$ across its range?' Unlike Partial Dependence Plots (which extrapolate into impossible feature combinations), ALE only computes effects within the *observed conditional distribution*.

Algorithm:
1. Divide feature $j$ into quantile bins
2. For each bin, compute the average change in prediction when moving feature $j$ from the bin's lower edge to its upper edge (holding other features at their observed values)
3. Accumulate these local effects
4. Center to zero mean

The result is a curve: x-axis is feature value, y-axis is marginal effect on prediction. Flat regions mean the feature doesn't matter at that range. Steep regions mean the model is sensitive there.

Look at the ALE for `log_atm_iv_0dte_d`: it's monotonically increasing -- higher implied vol predicts higher realized vol -- but the slope steepens above the 80th percentile. The model has learned that the *information content* of IV is higher in the tails."

## Part 5: QLIKE -- Why Not MSE? (3 min)

> **[SLIDE 13: QLIKE Loss -- back to slides]**
> **[📊 Then TOGGLE DASHBOARD: Rankings tab, point to the QLIKE column.]**

### The loss function

"Our primary metric is QLIKE -- the quasi-likelihood loss:

$$QLIKE = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \left[\frac{RV_t}{\hat{h}_t} - \log\frac{RV_t}{\hat{h}_t} - 1\right]$$

In log-space (how we compute it):
$$QLIKE = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \left[\exp(y_t - \hat{y}_t) - (y_t - \hat{y}_t) - 1\right]$$

This is minimized when $RV_t / \hat{h}_t = 1$ for all $t$. It measures *proportional* forecast error."

### Why not MSE?

"Three reasons:

**1. Scale invariance.** A 2× overestimate at 10% vol is the same penalty as a 2× overestimate at 40% vol. MSE penalizes absolute error -- so a few COVID observations completely dominate the entire metric. Using MSE, our model with ~0.3 Sharpe was selected because it happened to be less wrong in March 2020, not because it was systematically better.

**2. Economic alignment.** Underpredicting vol is catastrophically worse than overpredicting: you sell options too cheap, underestimate portfolio risk. QLIKE has a natural asymmetry -- a 2× underestimate scores 0.31, while a 2× overestimate scores 0.19. This matches the economic reality.

**3. Patton (2011) proxy robustness.** We never observe true integrated variance -- only realized variance, which is a noisy proxy. Patton proved that QLIKE produces *consistent model rankings* regardless of how noisy the proxy is. MSE rankings can *flip* depending on noise realization. This is a theorem -- not a heuristic.

LightGBM uses a custom QLIKE objective: we provide the gradient and Hessian of the QLIKE loss directly. The gradient is $g_i = 1 - \exp(y_i - \hat{y}_i)$ and the Hessian is $h_i = \exp(y_i - \hat{y}_i)$. This means the tree splits are optimized end-to-end for our actual loss function."

## Part 6: Out-of-Sample Testing and Purged CV (3 min)

> **[SLIDE 14: Purged Walk-Forward CV]**
> **[📊 Then TOGGLE DASHBOARD: Rankings tab. Note the OOS evaluation period in the table header.]**

### No lookahead bias

"Every number you see on this dashboard is **purely out-of-sample**. The model never sees the test data during training. But more importantly, the evaluation protocol has no *indirect* leakage:

1. **Temporal ordering**: training data is always *before* test data. No random shuffling.
2. **Purge gap**: 10 trading days between the last training observation and the first test observation. This accounts for the overlapping window in our target: $RV_{t+1}$ uses tomorrow's intraday returns, but our HAR features use today's close-to-close return which overlaps.
3. **Validation purge**: within each training fold, the early-stopping validation set also has a 10-day gap after the training split -- even the stopping criterion has no leakage."

### Expanding window walk-forward

"Our CV protocol is **expanding window** (walk-forward):
- Initial training window: 504 days (~2 years)
- Test window: 126 days (~6 months)
- Step forward by 126 days, expand training to include previous test data
- Purge gap: 10 days between train end and test start

This produces approximately 13–15 folds for our 2015–2026 history. The model is trained fresh on each fold -- there is no information accumulation across folds. The final metrics are the concatenation of all OOS predictions across folds.

For the 21-symbol pooled model, we use **panel-aware date splits**: the purge gap is computed in *dates*, not row indices, because multiple symbols share each date. We purge all symbols' observations within 10 dates of the test boundary."

### Multi-seed validation

"Single-seed results can be lucky. After trial-036 reported Sharpe-boosting h=1 QLIKE of 0.1289, we ran a 5-seed reseed (trial-047). The mean was 0.13679 -- the original was 79 bps below the 5-seed minimum. We now require multi-seed confirmation (≥3 seeds, report mean ± std) for all champion claims."

## Part 7: Statistical Tests (3 min)

> **[SLIDE 15: Statistical Tests]**
> **[📊 Then TOGGLE DASHBOARD: Look at DM Stat and MCS columns in the Rankings table]**

### Diebold-Mariano Test

"The DM test asks: 'Is model A's loss *significantly* lower than model B's?' We compute the loss differential series $d_t = L_A(t) - L_B(t)$ and test $H_0: E[d_t] = 0$.

The test statistic is:
$$DM = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\hat{V}(d)/T}}$$

where $\hat{V}(d)$ uses Newey-West HAC standard errors with Bartlett kernel (bandwidth = $h-1$ for horizon $h$) to account for serial correlation in multi-step forecast errors.

Under the null, $DM \sim N(0,1)$. A positive DM stat means model 2 (our model) has lower loss than the baseline. The p-value tells us the probability of seeing this improvement by chance.

> **[📊 ON DASHBOARD: Point to the dm_stat and dm_pvalue columns. Our LightGBM should show DM > 2 with p < 0.05 at h=1 and h=5]**

### Model Confidence Set (MCS)

"The MCS -- Hansen, Lunde & Nason (2011) -- identifies the *set* of models that contains the true best model with 90% confidence. It's a sequential elimination procedure:

1. Start with all models
2. Compute the range statistic $T_R = \max_{i,j}|t_{ij}|$ where $t_{ij}$ is the pairwise t-stat between models $i$ and $j$
3. Bootstrap $T_R$ under the null (block bootstrap, 10,000 replicates, block length $\sqrt{T}$)
4. If p-value < α (0.10): eliminate the worst model (highest max-t). Repeat.
5. Otherwise: all remaining models form the MCS.

Models in the MCS have a checkmark in the `mcs_included` column. If only our LightGBM + HAR-IV are in the MCS, it means we can reject (at 90% confidence) that any other model is as good.

### Mincer-Zarnowitz Regression

"The MZ test checks forecast *efficiency*:
$$RV_t = \alpha + \beta \cdot \hat{h}_t + \epsilon_t$$

An efficient forecast has $\alpha = 0, \beta = 1$ jointly. We test this with a Wald F-test using HAC standard errors. The `mz_beta` column shows calibration -- a beta below 1 means the forecast is too volatile (needs shrinkage), above 1 means it's underreacting. The `mz_f_pvalue` tests efficiency jointly."

## Part 8: GSVIVS01 Application (4 min)

> **[SLIDE 16: GSVIVS01 Signal]**
> **[📊 Then TOGGLE DASHBOARD: Click the GSVIVS tab. You'll see the signal performance table.]**

### What GSVIVS01 does

"GSVIVS01 is a daily variance swap replication on 0-DTE SPXW options. Every single day it:
- At 09:30 ET: sells a weighted strip of ~15 OTM options (95%–101% of forward) via 30-min TWAP
- Through the day: delta-hedges with ES futures (5-min clips, ~27/day)
- At 16:00 ET: all options settle at the MOC auction. Portfolio goes to cash.

The strip weights are $q_i \propto \Delta K_i / K_i^2$ -- this is the discrete approximation of a variance swap. The strategy has NO signal. It mechanically sells variance every day. It's a pure short-vol carry trade -- positive mean return (the VRP) but with left-tail drawdowns when realized vol exceeds implied.

Over 4 years (May 2022 – Jun 2026): index 100 → 138, roughly +9.6 pts/year."

### Our signal: the IV-RV gap

"We upgrade this into a directional strategy:
- If $K_{var} > \widehat{RV} + \theta$: go short variance (sell the strip) -- variance is overpriced
- If $K_{var} < \widehat{RV} - \theta$: go long variance (buy the strip) -- variance is underpriced
- $\theta = 0$ (binary signal) -- trial-042 showed any dead zone hurts

The signal fires at 09:10 ET, using:
- $\widehat{RV}$: our model's forecast for today's realized variance (made at last night's close, available well before open)
- $K_{var}$: the previous close's (~16:00 ET) fair variance swap strike from EDRVS

> **[📊 ON DASHBOARD: Point to the Sharpe column. H=1 should show Sharpe ~1.95 for our model vs ~1.6 for always-short]**

The edge is concentrated: only ~2% of days does the model predict RV > Kvar (signal = go long). Those days have 70% precision -- 7/10 correctly predicted GSVIVS drawdowns. That's where the Sharpe improvement comes from."

## Part 9: The Variance Swap Strike -- Kvar (3 min)

### Carr-Madan and model-free implied variance

"The correct 'implied vol' for our signal is not ATM IV -- it's the **variance swap strike**: the fair fixed leg of a variance swap. Carr and Madan (1998) showed this equals:

$$K_{var} = \frac{2}{T}\left[\int_0^F \frac{P(K)}{K^2}dK + \int_F^\infty \frac{C(K)}{K^2}dK\right]$$

where $F$ is the forward price, $P(K)$ and $C(K)$ are OTM put and call prices. The $1/K^2$ weighting gives more weight to OTM puts -- which have higher IV due to skew. So Kvar > ATM IV, always.

This formula is **model-free** -- it requires no parametric assumptions about the volatility surface. It falls directly out of the replication argument: a variance swap can be statically replicated by a portfolio of options weighted by $1/K^2$, plus dynamic delta-hedging.

The key mathematical fact: because the variance swap payoff $\sigma^2_{realized} - K_{var}$ is the integral of squared returns, and this payoff is *doubly differentiable* in the strike space, there exist traded options that exactly replicate it. The Carr-Madan formula simply prices that replicating portfolio."

### Our proxy situation

"In production, we would use **EDRVS_EXPIRY** from Marquee -- GS's internal variance swap fair value by listed expiry date. For SPX 0-DTE, this gives us the exact Kvar that GSVIVS sells against.

Currently in backtesting, we use a cached proxy (`gsvivs_kvar_daily.parquet`) derived from risk-node marks in the index JSON. ## Q&A Deep Dive: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)

### 1. Game-Theoretic Foundation: Shapley Values

SHAP is rooted in **cooperative game theory** -- specifically the Shapley value (Shapley 1953). Consider a cooperative game with $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, p\}$ players and a characteristic function $v: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}$ that assigns a value to each coalition (subset) of players. The **Shapley value** for player $i$ is:

$$\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|! \cdot (|N| - |S| - 1)!}{|N|!} \left[v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)\right]$$

**Term-by-term interpretation:**

- **$S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}$** -- a coalition of players that does *not* include player $i$. We sum over all $2^{p-1}$ possible coalitions.
- **$v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)$** -- the **marginal contribution** of player $i$ when joining coalition $S$. How much does player $i$ add to the coalition's value?
- **$\frac{|S|! \cdot (|N| - |S| - 1)!}{|N|!}$** -- the **weighting factor**. This is the probability of the particular ordering in which player $i$ arrives *after* exactly $|S|$ players have already joined.

**Why this specific weight?** Consider all $|N|!$ possible orderings (permutations) of the players arriving one at a time. For a given coalition $S$ of size $|S|$:
- There are $|S|!$ ways to arrange the $|S|$ players who arrive before $i$
- There are $(|N| - |S| - 1)!$ ways to arrange the remaining players who arrive after $i$
- So the fraction of permutations where exactly $S$ precedes $i$ is $\frac{|S|! \cdot (|N| - |S| - 1)!}{|N|!}$

Equivalently, the Shapley value is the **average marginal contribution of player $i$ across all possible arrival orderings**:

$$\phi_i = \frac{1}{|N|!} \sum_{\pi \in \Pi(N)} \left[v(S_i^\pi \cup \{i\}) - v(S_i^\pi)\right]$$

where $\Pi(N)$ is the set of all permutations of $N$, and $S_i^\pi$ is the set of players preceding $i$ in permutation $\pi$.

---

### 2. The Shapley Axioms -- Uniqueness Theorem

The Shapley value is the **unique** allocation satisfying four axioms simultaneously. No other allocation rule satisfies all four.

**Axiom 1 -- Efficiency (budget balance):**

$$\sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i = v(N) - v(\emptyset)$$

The individual attributions sum to the total value created by the grand coalition. Nothing is left over, nothing is double-counted. In ML terms: SHAP values for all features sum exactly to the difference between the prediction and the base value.

**Axiom 2 -- Symmetry:**

If players $i$ and $j$ are interchangeable -- i.e., $v(S \cup \{i\}) = v(S \cup \{j\})$ for all $S \subseteq N \setminus \{i, j\}$ -- then:

$$\phi_i = \phi_j$$

Players with identical marginal contributions everywhere receive equal attribution. No feature gets credit over another if their contributions are identical in all contexts.

**Axiom 3 -- Dummy (null player):**

If player $i$ contributes nothing to any coalition -- i.e., $v(S \cup \{i\}) = v(S)$ for all $S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}$ -- then:

$$\phi_i = 0$$

A feature that never changes the prediction receives zero attribution. Noise features are correctly zeroed out.

**Axiom 4 -- Linearity (additivity):**

If we have two games $v$ and $w$ with Shapley values $\phi^v$ and $\phi^w$, then for the combined game $v + w$:

$$\phi_i^{v+w} = \phi_i^v + \phi_i^w$$

This is what makes SHAP work for ensemble models: the SHAP values of a sum of trees equal the sum of SHAP values from each tree individually. For LightGBM with $T$ trees, we can compute SHAP per-tree and add them up.

**Uniqueness theorem (Shapley 1953):** There exists exactly one function $\phi: \mathcal{G}^N \to \mathbb{R}^N$ satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and linearity simultaneously. That function is the Shapley value formula above. Any deviation from Shapley values necessarily violates at least one axiom.

---

### 3. From Game Theory to ML: The SHAP Value Function

**Lundberg & Lee (2017)** connected Shapley values to ML model explanation by defining the cooperative game as follows:

- **Players** $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, p\}$ = the $p$ features of the model
- **Grand coalition value** $v(N) = f(x)$ = the model's prediction for observation $x$
- **Empty coalition value** $v(\emptyset) = E_X[f(X)]$ = the average prediction (base value)
- **Coalition value** $v(S) = E[f(X) \mid X_S = x_S]$ = the expected prediction when features in $S$ are fixed to their observed values and features *not* in $S$ are marginalized out

The value function $v(S)$ is the key modeling choice. It answers: "If we only know the values of features in $S$, what is our best prediction?" The answer is the conditional expectation:

$$v(S) = E_{X_{\bar{S}} | X_S = x_S}[f(X_S, X_{\bar{S}})]$$

where $\bar{S} = N \setminus S$ is the complement. We integrate out the unknown features over their conditional distribution given the known features.

**Important nuance:** Computing this conditional expectation exactly requires knowing $P(X_{\bar{S}} | X_S)$ -- the conditional distribution of missing features given observed ones. For correlated features, this is nontrivial. TreeSHAP offers two approaches:

1. **`tree_path_dependent`** (default): Uses the tree's internal data distribution to approximate the conditional. Each tree node encodes which training samples reached it -- the tree *is* a non-parametric conditional density estimator.

2. **`interventional`**: Sets $v(S) = E_{X_{\bar{S}}}[f(x_S, X_{\bar{S}})]$ -- marginalizes over the *marginal* (not conditional) distribution of $X_{\bar{S}}$, sampled from background data. This treats features as independent, which can overcount correlated features but gives "causal" interpretations.

For our LightGBM model, we use `tree_path_dependent` (the default in `shap.TreeExplainer`). This is appropriate because: (a) our features are highly correlated (e.g., `log_rv_d`, `log_rv_w`, `log_rv_m` share information), and (b) the path-dependent method exactly matches how the tree makes decisions.

---

### 4. TreeSHAP Algorithm (Lundberg et al. 2020)

#### The computational problem

Naively computing Shapley values requires evaluating $v(S)$ for all $2^p$ coalitions. With $p = 128$ features, that's $2^{128} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$ evaluations -- utterly intractable.

**KernelSHAP** (Lundberg & Lee 2017) approximates via weighted linear regression on a sample of coalitions, but it's still $O(2^p)$ in theory and noisy in practice.

#### TreeSHAP: exact polynomial-time computation

**TreeSHAP** (Lundberg et al. 2020, *Nature Machine Intelligence*) exploits the piecewise-constant structure of tree ensembles to compute **exact** Shapley values in polynomial time:

$$\text{Complexity} = O(T \cdot L \cdot D^2)$$

where:
- $T$ = number of trees (5000 max, but early stopping typically selects 300–800)
- $L$ = maximum leaves per tree (16 in our config)
- $D$ = maximum depth per tree (4 in our config)

For our model: $O(800 \times 16 \times 16) = O(204{,}800)$ operations per observation -- trivially fast.

#### How TreeSHAP works -- the algorithm

For a single decision tree, the algorithm performs a modified tree traversal:

1. **Start at the root.** Initialize a tracking structure that records, for each feature, whether it is "in the coalition" (value known) or "out" (marginalized).

2. **At each internal node** (splitting on feature $j$ at threshold $\tau$):
   - **If feature $j$ is IN the coalition:** Follow the single path consistent with $x_j$ (left if $x_j \leq \tau$, right if $x_j > \tau$). Feature $j$'s contribution is tracked.
   - **If feature $j$ is OUT of the coalition:** Follow BOTH branches, weighting each by the proportion of training samples that went left vs. right at this node. This is the marginalization step -- we average over the distribution of feature $j$ conditional on reaching this node.

3. **At each leaf:** The weighted leaf value contributes to the coalition value $v(S)$.

4. **The key recursion (EXTEND/UNWIND):** Rather than enumerating all $2^p$ coalitions explicitly, TreeSHAP maintains a polynomial-size representation of all possible coalitions simultaneously using a recursion over the "unique path" of features encountered from root to leaf. At each node, it *extends* the path (adds a feature to the tracking set) and upon backtracking *unwinds* it.

The combinatorial trick: for a path of depth $D$ through the tree, there are at most $D$ unique features involved. The Shapley weights over coalitions of these $D$ features can be computed exactly in $O(D^2)$ time using the EXTEND/UNWIND recursion on path fractions.

#### The key insight

Trees partition the feature space into axis-aligned rectangles (leaves). The conditional expectation $E[f(X) | X_S = x_S]$ for a tree is a weighted average of leaf values, where the weights are the fraction of training data in each leaf *consistent with the observed features in $S$*. Because trees make binary splits on individual features, "consistent with $X_S = x_S$" simply means: at every split on a feature in $S$, follow the observed direction. At every split on a feature NOT in $S$, follow both directions with data-frequency weights.

This recursive structure makes the summation over all $2^p$ coalitions collapse into a polynomial traversal -- the tree *is* the efficient computation structure for its own Shapley values.

---

### 5. SHAP Additivity -- Exact Decomposition

For any observation $i$, the SHAP values satisfy:

$$\hat{y}_i = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_{ij}$$

where:
- $\hat{y}_i$ = the model's prediction for observation $i$ (in our case, log-RV residual from the tree component)
- $\phi_0 = E_X[f(X)]$ = the base value (mean prediction over training data)
- $\phi_{ij}$ = SHAP value of feature $j$ for observation $i$

**This decomposition is EXACT for tree models.** There is no approximation error, no residual term, no confidence interval. Every prediction is perfectly decomposed into per-feature contributions. This is a mathematical consequence of the efficiency axiom applied to TreeSHAP's exact computation.

Summing across all features for a single observation: the feature contributions explain 100% of the deviation from the average prediction. There is no "unexplained" portion.

#### Subsampling for speed

- Full SHAP on all ~52,500 pooled observations would take ~10 minutes; 500 observations takes <2 seconds
- 500 is sufficient for stable mean |SHAP| rankings -- the top-10 features converge by ~200 samples

#### Global importance: mean |SHAP|

$$\text{Importance}_j = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |\phi_{ij}|$$

This gives the average absolute contribution of feature $j$ across observations. Unlike LightGBM's native `gain` importance (which counts how much each feature reduces the training loss at splits), mean |SHAP| is:
- Theoretically grounded (Shapley axioms guarantee fair attribution)
- Consistent across correlated features (gain double-counts; SHAP distributes fairly via the symmetry axiom)
- Instance-level decomposable (you can inspect any single prediction, not just the global average)

#### The beeswarm plot

- **x-axis:** SHAP value $\phi_{ij}$ -- the contribution to prediction for this observation. Positive = pushes log-RV prediction higher (more vol). Negative = pushes lower (less vol).
- **y-axis:** Features ranked by mean |SHAP| (most important at top)
- **Color:** Feature value (red = high quantile, blue = low quantile)
- **Each dot:** One observation. Horizontal spread shows the range of a feature's contributions.

Reading the plot: A feature with red dots clustered on the right and blue dots on the left has a *monotone positive* relationship with the prediction (higher feature value → higher vol forecast). Dispersed dots with mixed colors indicate interactions or nonlinearity.

#### Applied to RESIDUAL predictions

**Critical detail:** Our SHAP analysis explains what the **trees learned beyond HAR-IV**. The `init_score` (HAR-IV base prediction) is NOT part of the tree model -- it's subtracted before tree training begins. Therefore:

- SHAP values decompose the tree component: $f_{tree}(x_i) = \phi_0 + \sum_j \phi_{ij}$
- The total prediction is: $\hat{y}_i = \text{init\_score}_i + f_{tree}(x_i)$
- SHAP does NOT attribute anything to the HAR-IV features themselves -- it only explains what the 128 nonlinear features contribute *on top of* the linear base

This means high-SHAP features are those that capture **nonlinear structure that HAR-IV misses** -- interactions, regime-dependence, tail behavior, and calendar effects that a 4-parameter linear model cannot represent.

### 7. Interpreting SHAP for This Model

#### Direction of SHAP values

- **Positive SHAP** → pushes the tree's residual prediction higher → predicts MORE realized vol than HAR-IV alone would suggest
- **Negative SHAP** → pushes residual lower → predicts LESS vol than HAR-IV's estimate
- **SHAP ≈ 0** → feature is uninformative for this particular observation (may still matter on average)

#### What SHAP does NOT include

The `init_score` from HAR-IV carries the vast majority of predictive power (~90% of variance explained). SHAP values are the *marginal* contributions of the tree component, which accounts for the remaining ~10%. A feature with zero mean |SHAP| is not "unimportant" -- it may already be fully captured by the linear init_score.

#### Global importance interpretation

Mean |SHAP| gives a theoretically fair ranking of feature importance for the tree component. In our model, the top features are:

1. **IV×RV interactions** (`atm_iv_x_log_rv_d`) -- highest SHAP because the relationship between implied and realized vol is *regime-dependent*. In low-vol regimes, the VRP is stable and HAR-IV captures it linearly. In high-vol regimes, the VRP compresses nonlinearly -- the tree learns this interaction.

2. **VRP features** (`vrp_d`, `vrp_w`) -- the variance risk premium level carries residual information: when VRP is extremely high or extremely low, the tree adjusts the forecast. HAR-IV uses IV linearly but doesn't model VRP nonlinearity.

3. **Calendar features** (`days_to_fomc`, `opex_week`) -- pure nonlinear effects that HAR-IV has no way to capture. FOMC proximity creates a vol compression/release pattern that is inherently step-function-shaped.

4. **Z-score features** (`log_rv_d_zscore`, `vrp_d_zscore`) -- these flag "unusually high/low relative to recent history" which is a mean-reversion signal. The tree learns to mean-revert when the z-score is extreme, which HAR-IV cannot do (it's linear in levels, not in deviations from moving averages).

#### Individual SHAP reveals interactions

For a single observation where the tree predicts higher-than-average residual vol:
- If `atm_iv_x_log_rv_d` has large positive SHAP AND `vrp_d` also has positive SHAP → the model is detecting a regime where both the VRP interaction and the VRP level point toward more vol than the linear base expects. These features are *reinforcing*.
- If one is positive and the other negative → the model is detecting a mixed signal, and the net residual is smaller. SHAP reveals which features are fighting.

This per-observation decomposition is what makes SHAP superior to permutation importance or gain importance for model debugging: you can inspect *why* the model made a specific bad prediction and identify which feature was responsible.
