ML Vol Forecasting

Predicting Volatility

A machine-learned forecast of SPX realized variance, and one strategy it pays on

Ryan Vincent
Eqs STS Strats

Motivation and Project Scope
Overarching issue

Volatility is the input every strategy consumes: it prices the option, sizes the position, and decides which days a short-volatility book should not be short.

The premium for selling it accrues slowly and is handed back on the few days volatility spikes. The economics of the whole book therefore turn on seeing those days coming.

Goal

Develop a realized-volatility estimator that is interpretable: every term readable, every gain attributable. Not a black box that happens to backtest well.

This talk

Aim: beat the standard benchmark, and know why every feature earns its place.

Aim: keep the premium, skip the bad days, halve the drawdown.
The product and its problem

GSVIVS01 sells variance every single day

Each morning it sells a strip of same-day SPX options that replicates a variance swap, delta-hedges through the day, and settles at the close.

The gains are steady. The losses arrive on the few days realized variance beats the strike. See those days coming and we simply do not enter, avoiding the drawdown.

Part 1

Building the model to predict realized volatility

From the raw tape to a model that beats the benchmark and explains itself.

Building the forecast · the target

The number we train on

Supervised learning needs a label. For every day in the sample the model must be told what variance actually happened.

Liu, Patton and Sheppard tested about 400 estimators; five-minute RV is hard to beat.

Building the forecast · a sharper target

A reading one violent bar cannot spoil

Continuous variance is persistent, so it forecasts. Jumps are close to independent draws, so they do not. So the empirical RV we train on is upgraded to the day’s bipower reading.

jump-robust: a violent bar has no violent neighbour
Building the forecast · the windows

One day is a terrible sample size

Yesterday alone overreacts; a month is stable but slow to turn. So take all three, nested, all ending today. Everything sits in logs, because RV is positive and heavily right-skewed.

“… an additive cascade model of volatility components defined over different time periods.”

Corsi, “A Simple Approximate Long-Memory Model of Realized Volatility”, Journal of Financial Econometrics (2009) · day traders, weekly hedgers, monthly rebalancers: each horizon gets a term
Building the forecast · the forward-looking term

The one input that already knows about tomorrow

Every term so far is the past. Three windows of realized variance, and none of them knows there is an FOMC meeting this afternoon. The fourth term is the market’s own forecast: implied vol.

The detail that makes it work is tenor: each horizon reads the option that expires with it. An earlier version fed one-week IV into the one-day forecast and smuggled four days of term premium into every prediction; fixing that was one of the cleanest gains in the project.

That is the last term. Step back and look at what we have built.

Building the forecast · the spine

Four terms

Volatility clusters, so start from persistence: tomorrow’s variance, regressed on today’s.

One day is a noisy sample. The five-day average steadies it.

The month remembers the regime.

Three windows of the past, then one term that looks forward: same-day implied vol, tenor-matched.

“Despite extensive hyperparameter tuning, ML models fail to surpass the linear benchmark set by HAR …”

Audrino & Chassot, “HARd to Beat”, International Journal of Forecasting (2024) · 1,445 US stocks

This is HAR-IV, a desk classic. On its own: QLIKE 0.1617. That is the baseline benchmark, and everything from here is about beating it.

Building the forecast · the overlay

Let the trees learn only what the spine gets wrong

XGBoost is a gradient boosted decision tree: a sequence of weak learners, each a small tree fitted to the residual of everything before it. Here the sequence does not start from zero. It starts from the spine.

Why the spine is the start, not a feature
Trees cannot extrapolate
A tree's output is a step function over its training range: fed the spine's forecast as a column, the trees would still have to produce the level, and would clip on days outside training. Starting from the spine, the level comes from a linear model that extrapolates; the trees shape only bounded corrections.
The floor is the spine
If the trees find nothing, the forecast is exactly the spine, whose four parameters remain readable. Every deviation has to be earned on QLIKE, the loss the forecast is judged by.
Why trust the number

Walk-forward with a moat, three seeds

Training always ends ten trading days before testing begins, on every fold, because the target itself overlaps days. Splits are by date across all 100 symbols, so no symbol leaks the future to another. Even the early-stopping check sits behind its own gap.

What it learned

Making the black-box interpretable

SHAP splits every individual forecast into named feature contributions that sum exactly to the prediction, so we can audit what the trees add on top of the linear spine.

What tops the list: the implied-to-realized relationship changing with regime, extremes of the variance risk premium, Fed-meeting proximity, and flags for readings unusually high against their own history.

What it learned · the shortlist

Five that don’t repeat each other

Twelve features top the one-day SHAP ranking; the other seven are the same objects seen again: vix_x_log_rv_d_change and _m_change differenced or taken monthly, vvix_d and vvix_innovation_d sitting behind the risk premium, iv_skew_w behind the front-end slope. Two are genuinely separate: noise_gap_w, the realized-kernel gap from slide 5, and vol_anomaly, today's tick count against its 22-day mean.

What it learned · the response curves

ALE: the shape behind each name

SHAP says what mattered. ALE says what shape the response takes, one input at a time, with everything else held honest.

Vertical axis is the effect on the one-day log-variance forecast; each panel is scaled to its own range, so read the shapes against each other and the printed extents for size. Three of the four fall to the right: the model is pricing mean reversion off stretched readings, not momentum.

The claim

Every morning: compare the forecast to the strike

At 09:10, before the strip is sold, the model's overnight forecast of today's realized variance is compared with the strike on offer. Variance rich: sell as usual. Forecast above the strike: stand aside for the day.

Results

Where it wins, and what it costs

Against plain HAR the trees cut forecast loss by 23.4%: QLIKE 0.1617 down to 0.1239, R² 0.664 up to 0.734, Diebold-Mariano p = 0.003. And it helps most where it matters: in turbulent periods HAR scores 0.2068 and the trees 0.1362, a 34.2% gain against 21.7% in calm markets.

On the strategy that is Sharpe 2.57 against 2.06 for selling every day without looking, over 962 days.

Now the part that is easy to oversell. Always-selling earns +38.0% over the window; standing aside earns +35.0%. The signal costs three points of return. Sharpe rises because annual vol falls from 4.1% to 3.1% and the worst drawdown halves, from 5.1% to 2.5%. I am not claiming alpha. I am claiming the same carry with half the tail.

Every headline number is a three-seed mean.

The last thing I tried

Ninety-one percent of it was a straight line

Having built the trees, I spent the rest of the project trying to beat them without any. Five rungs, each a hypothesis with a gate in front of it: an FOMC-day residual bump, a VIX-regime split, a piecewise slope for the volume anomaly, another for the weekly skew, then rolling z-scores so a fixed-coefficient regression sees a state-normalised regressor. Every arm fits under a strict-features guard, so a regressor that silently goes missing fails loudly instead of reporting a fake null.

HAR to the trees is worth 23.4% of forecast loss. Seventeen linear regressors recover 91% of it, 21.2% of the 23.4%, and the same 91% on Sharpe.
