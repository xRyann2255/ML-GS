\chapter{Microstructure Noise and Robust Estimators}
\label{ch:noise}

\begin{application}[Why This Chapter]
Microstructure noise is the reason you cannot simply sample tick-by-tick and compute realized variance.
This chapter teaches when noise matters, how it biases RV, and the family of robust estimators that correct for it.
Chapter~\ref{ch:features} uses these estimators as features.
Project~2 (LOB-based intraday) works directly in the high-frequency regime where noise is most severe.
\end{application}

Chapter~\ref{ch:rv} ended with a paradox.
The theory says ``sample as fast as possible'': more intraday returns means a more precise estimate of integrated variance.
But the volatility signature plot showed that sampling faster than about 5 minutes makes the estimate \emph{worse}, not better, because microstructure noise inflates the sum of squared returns.
This chapter explains exactly where that noise comes from, quantifies the damage it does, and develops four estimators that fix the problem: TSRV, MSRV, the realized kernel, and pre-averaging.

\section{The Microstructure Noise Problem}
\label{sec:noise-problem}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

Chapter~\ref{ch:rv} introduced the noise model $p^*_{t,i} = p_{t,i} + \varepsilon_{t,i}$ (Equation~\ref{eq:noise-model}) and showed that noise causes RV to diverge.
This section digs into \emph{where} the noise comes from, because understanding the source tells you when it matters and when it does not.

\begin{prereq}[Bid, Ask, Mid, and Spread]
At any moment, a stock has two prices: the \emph{bid} (the highest price a buyer will pay) and the \emph{ask} (the lowest price a seller will accept).
The \emph{mid price} is the average, $(P_{\text{bid}} + P_{\text{ask}})/2$.
The \emph{spread} is $P_{\text{ask}} - P_{\text{bid}}$.
Transaction prices alternate between bid and ask depending on who initiates the trade (a market buy hits the ask; a market sell hits the bid).
\end{prereq}

\begin{prereq}[Sampling Frequency Terminology]
``Sampling at 1-second frequency'' means computing one return per second.
A 6.5-hour U.S.\ equity trading day has $6.5 \times 3{,}600 = 23{,}400$ one-second intervals.
``Tick-by-tick'' means using every single trade as a price observation; for liquid stocks, this can mean thousands of observations per minute.
\end{prereq}

There are three main sources of microstructure noise.

\paragraph{Source 1: Bid-ask bounce.}
Even if the true value of a stock is perfectly constant at \$50.00, alternating buys and sells produce transaction prices that bounce between \$49.99 (bid) and \$50.01 (ask).
Computing returns from these prices generates a sequence of $+0.02$, $-0.02$, $+0.02$, $\ldots$ that has nothing to do with actual volatility.
Squaring and summing these fake returns inflates RV.
Bid-ask bounce is the dominant source of noise for most liquid assets \citep{HansenLunde2006}.

\paragraph{Source 2: Discrete tick sizes.}
Prices on exchanges are quoted in discrete increments (one cent for U.S.\ equities).
When the true efficient price is \$50.003, the observed price must round to either \$50.00 or \$50.01.
This rounding adds a small, mean-zero error to every observation.
At very high frequencies, these rounding errors accumulate into a non-trivial noise term.

\paragraph{Source 3: Price staleness.}
Illiquid stocks may not trade every second.
If you sample the ``last trade price'' at regular intervals, you often repeat the same stale price.
The resulting return is zero, which underestimates true volatility.
When a new trade finally arrives, the return is artificially large.
This creates spurious autocorrelation in returns \citep{AitSahaliaMyklandZhang2005}.

\begin{intuition}[Why Noise Kills High-Frequency RV]
At very high frequencies, observed prices alternate between bid and ask.
Squaring these back-and-forth moves inflates the sum far beyond the true volatility.
The signal (real price moves) scales with the time interval $\Delta$, but the noise (bid-ask bounce) has a roughly constant variance $\omega^2$ per observation.
As you shrink $\Delta$, the noise-to-signal ratio explodes: each return is mostly noise, and you are summing $n = 1/\Delta$ of them.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Quantifying the Bias}

The standard noise model assumes the observed log price is the true (efficient) price plus i.i.d.\ noise:
\[
  p^*_{t,i} = p_{t,i} + \varepsilon_{t,i}, \qquad \varepsilon_{t,i} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} (0, \omega^2)
\]

\citet{HansenLunde2006} and \citet{AitSahaliaMyklandZhang2005} showed that, under this model, the expectation of RV computed from noisy prices is:

\begin{equation}
  \E[\RV_t^{(\text{noisy})}] = \IVol_t + 2n\omega^2
  \label{eq:rv-noise-bias}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\RV_t^{(\text{noisy})}$: realized variance computed from observed (noisy) prices
  \item $\IVol_t$: true integrated variance (the target)
  \item $n$: number of intraday returns
  \item $\omega^2$: variance of the microstructure noise per observation
  \item $2n\omega^2$: the noise-induced bias, linear in $n$
\end{itemize}

The bias $2n\omega^2$ grows linearly with the number of observations.
As $n \to \infty$ (tick-by-tick), the bias term dominates and $\RV_t^{(\text{noisy})} \to \infty$.
The $\IVol_t$ term becomes a negligible fraction of the total.

\begin{warning}[The Bias Is Not Just Upward]
The i.i.d.\ noise model predicts a purely upward bias.
In practice, noise can also be negatively autocorrelated (alternating buys and sells) or positively autocorrelated (momentum in order flow), which can shift the bias in either direction.
\citet{HansenLunde2006} found that the simple i.i.d.\ model is a useful first approximation for liquid stocks, but the actual noise structure is more complex.
Always inspect the volatility signature plot for your specific asset.
\end{warning}

\section{The Volatility Signature Plot}
\label{sec:vol-sig-plot}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

Chapter~\ref{ch:rv} introduced the volatility signature plot (Figure~\ref{fig:vol-sig-plot}).
This section explains it as a \emph{diagnostic tool}: the first thing you should plot before choosing an estimator or sampling frequency.

\begin{definition}[Volatility Signature Plot (Formal)]
The volatility signature plot displays $\bar{\RV}(\Delta)$, the average realized variance computed across $T$ days, as a function of the sampling interval $\Delta$:
\[
  \bar{\RV}(\Delta) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \RV_t(\Delta)
\]
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\Delta$: sampling interval (e.g., 1 second, 1 minute, 5 minutes, $\ldots$)
  \item $\RV_t(\Delta)$: realized variance for day $t$ computed at interval $\Delta$
  \item $T$: number of trading days in the sample
\end{itemize}
The $x$-axis shows $\Delta$ (often on a log scale), and the $y$-axis shows $\bar{\RV}(\Delta)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{keyidea}[The Volatility Signature Plot Is a Diagnostic, Not a Conclusion]
The shape of the volatility signature plot tells you where noise begins to bite for your specific asset.
Before computing RV for any analysis, plot $\bar{\RV}(\Delta)$ for a range of frequencies.
If the curve is still rising at your chosen frequency, you are in the noise-contaminated region and must either reduce frequency or use a noise-robust estimator from this chapter.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{warning}[The Sweet Spot Varies by Asset]
The 5-minute rule of thumb holds for liquid large-cap equities and major FX pairs.
For less liquid instruments (small-cap stocks, emerging-market currencies, corporate bonds), the noise-contaminated region can extend to 15 or even 30 minutes \citep{HansenLunde2006}.
For extremely liquid futures (e.g., E-mini S\&P~500), the curve may flatten by 1 minute.
Always check empirically.
\end{warning}

\section{Two-Scales Realized Volatility (TSRV)}
\label{sec:tsrv}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

The volatility signature plot shows that standard RV is badly biased at high frequencies.
One approach (Chapter~\ref{ch:rv}) is to avoid the problem by sampling at 5 minutes.
A better approach is to \emph{correct} for the noise and use all the high-frequency data.
The Two-Scales Realized Volatility (TSRV) estimator, developed by \citet{ZhangMyklandAitSahalia2005}, is the simplest noise-robust estimator.

\begin{intuition}[The Two-Scales Idea]
Compute RV at two different frequencies: a ``fast'' scale (e.g., every 1 minute) and a ``slow'' scale (e.g., every 30 minutes).
The fast-scale RV picks up both signal and noise: $\RV^{(\text{fast})} \approx \IVol_t + 2n_{\text{fast}}\omega^2$.
The slow-scale RV also picks up signal and noise, but with far fewer observations: $\RV^{(\text{slow})} \approx \IVol_t + 2n_{\text{slow}}\omega^2$.
However, the key insight is that the \emph{noise per observation} is the same at both scales.
By taking a specific weighted difference of the two, you can cancel the noise term and isolate the signal.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Construction}

TSRV works with subsampled realized variances.
Instead of computing a single RV on a non-overlapping grid, you average over all possible starting points.

\begin{prereq}[Subsampling]
A ``subsampled'' RV at scale $K$ takes the original $n$ tick prices and computes $K$ separate realized variances, each on a non-overlapping grid offset by one tick.
For example, with $K = 3$, you compute RV using ticks $\{1, 4, 7, \ldots\}$, then $\{2, 5, 8, \ldots\}$, then $\{3, 6, 9, \ldots\}$, and average the three.
This ``average RV'' uses all the data while spacing returns $K$ ticks apart.
\end{prereq}

Denote the subsampled (averaged) RV at scale $K$ as $\RV^{(\text{avg}, K)}_t$.
TSRV uses a fast scale $K = 1$ (every tick) and a slow scale $K = K_{\text{slow}}$ (spaced-out returns):

\begin{definition}[Two-Scales Realized Volatility (TSRV)]
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\IVol}_t^{\text{TSRV}} = \RV^{(\text{avg}, K_{\text{slow}})}_t - \frac{\bar{n}_{K_\text{slow}}}{n}\, \RV^{(\text{all})}_t
  \label{eq:tsrv}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\RV^{(\text{avg}, K_{\text{slow}})}_t$: the subsampled (averaged) RV computed with returns spaced $K_{\text{slow}}$ ticks apart
  \item $\RV^{(\text{all})}_t$: realized variance computed from all $n$ tick-by-tick returns (the ``fast scale'')
  \item $\bar{n}_{K_\text{slow}} = (n - K_{\text{slow}} + 1)/K_{\text{slow}}$: the average number of returns per subsample at the slow scale
  \item $n$: total number of tick-level returns
  \item The ratio $\bar{n}_{K_\text{slow}} / n$ is a small number; the second term removes the noise bias from the first
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{intuition}[Why the Subtraction Works]
Both $\RV^{(\text{avg}, K_{\text{slow}})}_t$ and $\RV^{(\text{all})}_t$ contain a noise bias proportional to $2 \omega^2 \times (\text{number of returns})$.
The subsampled slow-scale RV has bias $\approx 2\bar{n}_{K_\text{slow}}\omega^2$.
The all-tick RV has bias $\approx 2n\omega^2$.
Subtracting $(\bar{n}_{K_\text{slow}} / n) \times \RV^{(\text{all})}_t$ removes exactly $2\bar{n}_{K_\text{slow}}\omega^2$ from the slow-scale estimate, canceling the noise.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Convergence Rate}

\begin{keyresult}[\citet{ZhangMyklandAitSahalia2005}: TSRV Convergence]
Under the i.i.d.\ noise model, the optimal choice of $K_{\text{slow}}$ yields a convergence rate of $n^{-1/6}$ for the TSRV estimator:
\[
  \widehat{\IVol}_t^{\text{TSRV}} - \IVol_t = O_p(n^{-1/6})
\]
For comparison, the standard 5-minute RV (without noise correction) converges at rate $n^{-1/2}$ but only \emph{if you ignore noise}.
With noise, standard RV does not converge at all.
TSRV is the first estimator that is consistent in the presence of noise.
\end{keyresult}

The $n^{-1/6}$ rate is slower than the noise-free $n^{-1/2}$.
This is the price you pay for not knowing the noise level $\omega^2$ a priori.
Subsequent estimators (MSRV, realized kernel) improve this rate.

\begin{workedexample}{TSRV on a 6.5-Hour Trading Day}
Suppose you have tick-by-tick data for a liquid stock with $n = 23{,}400$ ticks (one per second over 6.5 hours).
You choose a slow scale of $K_{\text{slow}} = 390$ (equivalent to spacing returns 390 seconds, or 6.5 minutes, apart).

\textbf{Step 1: Fast-scale RV.}
Compute $\RV^{(\text{all})}_t$ from all 23,400 one-second returns.
Say you get $\RV^{(\text{all})}_t = 3.2 \times 10^{-4}$.
This is inflated by noise.

\textbf{Step 2: Slow-scale subsampled RV.}
Compute 390 separate RVs, each from a non-overlapping grid of $23{,}400 / 390 = 60$ returns spaced 390 seconds apart, offset by one tick each time.
Average them: $\RV^{(\text{avg}, 390)}_t = 1.3 \times 10^{-4}$.

\textbf{Step 3: Noise correction.}
The average number of returns per subsample is $\bar{n}_{390} = (23{,}400 - 390 + 1)/390 \approx 59$.
The correction factor is $\bar{n}_{390}/n = 59/23{,}400 \approx 0.00252$.

\textbf{Step 4: Combine.}
\[
  \widehat{\IVol}_t^{\text{TSRV}} = 1.3 \times 10^{-4} - 0.00252 \times 3.2 \times 10^{-4} = 1.3 \times 10^{-4} - 0.008 \times 10^{-4} \approx 1.29 \times 10^{-4}
\]

The correction is small here because the slow scale already removes most noise.
In cases where the slow scale is less aggressive (e.g., $K_{\text{slow}} = 30$), the correction is larger.
The annualized volatility is $\sqrt{1.29 \times 10^{-4}} \times \sqrt{252} \approx 18.0\%$.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Multi-Scale Realized Volatility (MSRV)}
\label{sec:msrv}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

TSRV uses two scales.
A natural extension is to use \emph{many} scales simultaneously.
\citet{Zhang2006} developed the Multi-Scale Realized Volatility (MSRV) estimator, which combines information from multiple time scales to achieve a faster convergence rate.

\begin{intuition}[From Two Scales to Many]
Think of TSRV as using a single pair of binoculars: one lens zoomed in (fast scale) and one zoomed out (slow scale).
MSRV uses an entire array of zoom levels, weighting each one optimally.
More zoom levels means more information about how the noise distorts each scale, which means a more precise correction.
\end{intuition}

\begin{definition}[Multi-Scale Realized Volatility (MSRV)]
MSRV takes a weighted combination of subsampled RVs at $J$ different scales $K_1 < K_2 < \cdots < K_J$:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\IVol}_t^{\text{MSRV}} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} a_j \, \RV^{(\text{avg}, K_j)}_t
  \label{eq:msrv}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\RV^{(\text{avg}, K_j)}_t$: subsampled RV at scale $K_j$ (same construction as in TSRV)
  \item $a_j$: weights chosen to (i) cancel the noise bias and (ii) minimize variance
  \item $J$: number of scales; \citet{Zhang2006} showed that the optimal $J$ grows with $n$
  \item The weights satisfy $\sum_j a_j = 1$ (so the estimator targets $\IVol_t$) and a second constraint that cancels the $\omega^2$ bias
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{keyresult}[\citet{Zhang2006}: MSRV Achieves the Optimal Rate]
The MSRV estimator achieves the convergence rate $n^{-1/4}$:
\[
  \widehat{\IVol}_t^{\text{MSRV}} - \IVol_t = O_p(n^{-1/4})
\]
This is the best possible rate for any estimator under the i.i.d.\ noise model without knowing $\omega^2$.
For context: with $n = 23{,}400$ one-second observations, $n^{-1/4} \approx 0.081$, compared to $n^{-1/6} \approx 0.188$ for TSRV.
MSRV is roughly 2.3 times more precise than TSRV for the same data.
\end{keyresult}

In practice, MSRV is straightforward to implement (it is a weighted sum of subsampled RVs), but the optimal weight selection requires choosing $J$ and the scale grid $\{K_j\}$.
\citet{Zhang2006} provides explicit formulas.
For most applied work, the realized kernel (next section) is preferred because it achieves the same $n^{-1/4}$ rate with a simpler tuning procedure.

\section{The Realized Kernel}
\label{sec:realized-kernel}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

The realized kernel, developed by \citet{BNHLS2008}, is the most widely used noise-robust estimator in the volatility literature.
It achieves the optimal $n^{-1/4}$ convergence rate (same as MSRV) with a clean, intuitive construction.

\begin{intuition}[Noise as Spurious Autocorrelation]
Under the i.i.d.\ noise model, the \emph{observed} returns $r^*_{t,i}$ have negative first-order autocorrelation: if the noise pushes the price up this tick, the next return is more likely to be negative (reverting the noise).
This spurious autocorrelation shows up in the autocovariances of returns.
The realized kernel idea: compute the autocovariance function of observed returns and use a kernel weighting to remove the noise-induced autocorrelation while keeping the genuine signal.
\end{intuition}

\begin{prereq}[Autocovariance]
The autocovariance at lag $h$ of a sequence $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ measures the linear association between observations $h$ steps apart:
\[
  \hat{\gamma}_h = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n-h} (x_i - \bar{x})(x_{i+h} - \bar{x})
\]
For returns, lag 0 autocovariance is the variance.
Lag 1 autocovariance measures whether today's return predicts tomorrow's (or this tick's return predicts the next).
\end{prereq}

\begin{prereq}[Kernel Functions]
A kernel function $k(x)$ is a smooth weighting function satisfying $k(0) = 1$ and $k(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$.
It assigns full weight to the center (lag~0) and smoothly downweights observations further away.
Common examples: the Bartlett kernel $k(x) = 1 - |x|$ for $|x| \leq 1$ (a triangle), and the Parzen kernel (a piecewise cubic that is smoother at the boundary).
\end{prereq}

\subsection{Construction}

The realized kernel takes the autocovariances of observed returns and weights them with a kernel function.

\begin{definition}[Realized Kernel]
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{K}_t = \sum_{h=-H}^{H} k\!\left(\frac{h}{H+1}\right) \hat{\gamma}_h
  \label{eq:realized-kernel}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\hat{\gamma}_h = \sum_{i=1}^{n-|h|} r^*_{t,i}\, r^*_{t,i+|h|}$: the $h$-th sample autocovariance of observed (noisy) returns; note $\hat{\gamma}_0 = \RV_t^{(\text{noisy})}$
  \item $k(\cdot)$: a kernel function with $k(0) = 1$, typically the Parzen kernel
  \item $H$: bandwidth (number of lags included); controls the bias-variance tradeoff
  \item The sum runs from $-H$ to $H$, so both positive and negative lags are included
  \item $k(h/(H+1))$: the weight assigned to autocovariance at lag $h$; lags near zero get high weight, distant lags get low weight
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The ``flat-top'' property is important: \citet{BNHLS2008} require $k'(0) = 0$ (the kernel is flat at the origin), which ensures that the noise bias is removed at the correct rate.
The Parzen kernel satisfies this.

Figure~\ref{fig:kernel-weights} shows how the Parzen kernel assigns weights to different lags.

\subsection{Why It Works}

The logic is:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Lag 0}: The zero-lag autocovariance $\hat{\gamma}_0 = \RV_t^{(\text{noisy})}$ contains the signal plus noise bias.
  \item \textbf{Lags 1, 2, \ldots}: The noise-induced autocorrelation appears at lag $\pm 1$ (and possibly a few more lags for dependent noise). These autocovariances are negative on average, reflecting the bid-ask bounce reversal.
  \item \textbf{Kernel weighting}: By including these negative autocovariances with appropriate weights, their sum partially cancels the positive noise bias in $\hat{\gamma}_0$.
  \item \textbf{Higher lags}: Beyond a few lags, the noise-induced autocorrelation dies out, and genuine autocorrelation (if any) is captured.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keyidea}[The Realized Kernel in Practice]
The realized kernel is the most widely used noise-robust estimator in empirical finance.
It achieves the optimal convergence rate ($n^{-1/4}$), handles moderately dependent noise (not just i.i.d.), has well-understood asymptotic theory, and is implemented in standard packages (\texttt{highfrequency} in R, \texttt{realized} in MATLAB).
If you need a noise-robust daily RV estimate, the realized kernel is the default choice.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Bandwidth Selection}

The bandwidth $H$ controls how many autocovariance lags are included.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Too small $H$}: not enough lags to fully remove the noise bias; the estimator is biased upward.
  \item \textbf{Too large $H$}: too many noisy autocovariance estimates are included; the estimator has high variance.
\end{itemize}

\citet{BNHLS2008} showed that the optimal bandwidth scales as $H^* \propto n^{3/5}$.
For $n = 23{,}400$ one-second observations, this gives $H^* \approx 23{,}400^{3/5} \approx 400$.
Data-driven bandwidth selection methods (analogous to those used in spectral density estimation) are available and recommended for applied work.

\section{Pre-Averaging}
\label{sec:pre-averaging}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

Pre-averaging, developed by \citet{JacodLiMyklandPodolskijVetter2009}, takes a different approach to the noise problem.
Instead of correcting the autocovariance structure (as the realized kernel does), it smooths the noise out of the price path \emph{before} computing squared returns.

\begin{intuition}[Smoothing Out the Bounce]
If bid-ask bounce makes individual prices noisy, averaging a few adjacent prices should cancel some of the noise (positive and negative noise terms offset).
Pre-averaging does exactly this: replace each price $p^*_{t,i}$ with a local average of nearby prices, then compute returns and sum their squares.
The local averaging shrinks the noise while preserving the signal.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Construction}

\begin{definition}[Pre-Averaged Realized Variance]
Choose a block length $L$ and a weight function $g:[0,1] \to \R$ (typically $g(x) = \min(x, 1-x)$).
Define the pre-averaged price:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{p}^*_{t,i} = \sum_{j=1}^{L-1} g\!\left(\frac{j}{L}\right) \Delta p^*_{t,i+j}
  \label{eq:pre-avg-price}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta p^*_{t,i+j} = p^*_{t,i+j} - p^*_{t,i+j-1}$ is the tick-by-tick return.
The pre-averaged realized variance is:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\IVol}_t^{\text{PA}} = \frac{1}{L \psi_2} \sum_{i=0}^{n - L} (\bar{p}^*_{t,i})^2 - \frac{\psi_1}{2 L \psi_2}\, \RV^{(\text{all})}_t
  \label{eq:pre-avg-rv}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\bar{p}^*_{t,i}$: the pre-averaged return at position $i$ (a weighted sum of $L-1$ tick returns)
  \item $g(j/L)$: weight function; the standard choice $g(x) = \min(x, 1-x)$ gives a triangular shape that ramps up then ramps down
  \item $\psi_1 = \int_0^1 [g'(x)]^2\,dx$ and $\psi_2 = \int_0^1 [g(x)]^2\,dx$: constants determined by the weight function
  \item $\RV^{(\text{all})}_t$: tick-by-tick RV (used for a small bias correction, analogous to the TSRV correction)
  \item $L$: block length; the optimal choice is $L \propto n^{1/2}$
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\section{Other Estimators}
\label{sec:other-estimators}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

The four estimators above (TSRV, MSRV, realized kernel, pre-averaging) are the most cited.
Several other approaches exist and are worth brief mention.

\paragraph{Subsampled/Averaged RV.}
Before TSRV was developed, practitioners used averaged RV (computing multiple RVs on offset grids and averaging them) to reduce noise.
This is the slow-scale component of TSRV without the bias correction.
It reduces noise variance but does not eliminate the bias.
It remains a useful quick fix when the noise level is low.

\paragraph{Fourier Estimator.}
\citet{MalliavinMancino2002, MalliavinMancino2009} proposed estimating integrated variance via the Fourier coefficients of the price process.
The idea: the variance is related to the squared magnitude of low-frequency Fourier coefficients, while noise concentrates at high frequencies.
By truncating high-frequency components, you filter out the noise.
The Fourier estimator is less widely used in practice but has elegant theoretical properties and does not require equally spaced observations.

\paragraph{Quasi-Maximum Likelihood Estimator (QMLE).}
\citet{Xiu2010} proposed treating the noisy price observations as a state-space model: the true price is the latent state, and the observed price adds Gaussian noise.
Applying the Kalman filter produces a quasi-maximum likelihood estimate of integrated variance and the noise variance $\omega^2$ jointly.
The QMLE achieves the optimal $n^{-1/4}$ rate and provides a natural estimate of $\omega^2$ as a byproduct.

\begin{keyidea}[All Roads Lead to $n^{-1/4}$]
Under the standard i.i.d.\ noise model, the best possible convergence rate is $n^{-1/4}$.
All of the modern estimators (MSRV, realized kernel, pre-averaging, QMLE) achieve this rate.
They differ in implementation details, robustness to dependent noise, and ease of tuning, but their asymptotic efficiency is the same.
\end{keyidea}

\section*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Three noise sources}: bid-ask bounce (dominant for liquid assets), discrete tick sizes, and price staleness each corrupt high-frequency prices.

  \item \textbf{Noise model}: observed log price $p^* = p + \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is mean-zero noise with variance $\omega^2$. Under this model, noisy RV has bias $2n\omega^2$, which diverges as $n \to \infty$.

  \item \textbf{Volatility signature plot}: plot average RV vs.\ sampling frequency. If the curve is still rising at your chosen frequency, you are in the noise-contaminated region. Always check this diagnostic before computing RV.

  \item \textbf{TSRV} \citep{ZhangMyklandAitSahalia2005}: combines a fast-scale and slow-scale RV to cancel noise. First consistent estimator with noise. Convergence rate $n^{-1/6}$.

  \item \textbf{MSRV} \citep{Zhang2006}: extends TSRV to multiple scales, achieving the optimal convergence rate $n^{-1/4}$.

  \item \textbf{Realized kernel} \citep{BNHLS2008}: weights the autocovariances of observed returns with a flat-top kernel (e.g., Parzen). Handles dependent noise. Rate $n^{-1/4}$. The most widely used noise-robust estimator.

  \item \textbf{Pre-averaging} \citep{JacodLiMyklandPodolskijVetter2009}: smooths prices over local blocks before computing squared returns. Rate $n^{-1/4}$. Provides a feasible CLT for inference.

  \item \textbf{Other approaches}: subsampled/averaged RV (simple but biased), Fourier estimator \citep{MalliavinMancino2002}, QMLE \citep{Xiu2010}.

  \item \textbf{All optimal estimators converge at $n^{-1/4}$} under i.i.d.\ noise. They differ in robustness to dependent noise, implementation complexity, and tuning requirements.

  \item \textbf{For forecasting}, 5-minute RV is hard to beat \citep{LPS2015}. Noise-robust estimators improve \emph{estimation} accuracy but these gains rarely translate into forecast improvements.

  \item \textbf{For estimation and intraday work}, the realized kernel is the default choice. Pre-averaging is a close second.

  \item Chapter~\ref{ch:jumps} addresses a separate contamination: jump variation mixed into the continuous component.

  \item Chapter~\ref{ch:features} uses both 5-minute RV and noise-robust estimators as ML features.
\end{itemize}
