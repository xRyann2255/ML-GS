\chapter{Volatility Spillovers and Connectedness}\label{ch:spillovers}

\begin{application}
Chapter~\ref{ch:multivariate} built tools for forecasting the full covariance matrix.
This chapter focuses on a specific question: how does volatility transmit from one
asset (or market) to another?  Spillover indices and connectedness measures are both
diagnostic tools (understanding contagion) and predictive features
(Chapter~\ref{ch:features}).  Project~3 relies heavily on this framework.
\end{application}

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Diebold--Yilmaz Spillover Indices}\label{sec:dy-spillover}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

In Chapter~\ref{ch:multivariate} you learned to model the joint dynamics of multiple
assets.  Now we zoom in on a sharper question: when one asset's volatility jumps,
how much of that shock spills over into other assets?

\begin{intuition}[Volatility as a contagious shock]
Think of volatility shocks as ripples in a pond.  A stone dropped on the equity
side sends waves that reach bonds, commodities, and currencies.  The Diebold--Yilmaz
(DY) framework measures the size and direction of those ripples using standard
vector autoregression (VAR) machinery.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Setup: VAR on Realized Volatilities}

Stack the realized volatilities of $N$ assets into a vector
$\by_t = (\RV_{1,t}, \ldots, \RV_{N,t})'$ and fit a VAR($p$):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-rv}
  \by_t = \bm{c} + \sum_{\ell=1}^{p} \bm{A}_\ell \, \by_{t-\ell} + \bm{u}_t,
  \qquad \bm{u}_t \sim (0, \bm{\Sigma}),
\end{equation}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
Today's vector of realized volatilities across all assets is a linear combination of
the past $p$ days' volatility vectors, plus a surprise term.  Each coefficient matrix
$\bm{A}_\ell$ captures how yesterday's (or day-before's) vol in \emph{every} asset
feeds into today's vol for \emph{every} asset.  This is the multivariate extension
of the HAR idea: past vol predicts future vol, but now cross-asset effects are
explicitly modeled.
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
The VAR on RVs is the backbone of the Diebold-Yilmaz framework.  For your project,
the off-diagonal entries of $\bm{A}_\ell$ are cross-asset predictive features: they
tell you how much of asset $j$'s vol is predictable from asset $k$'s recent vol.
This directly extends the univariate HAR baseline with cross-asset lags.
\end{projectconnection}

where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\by_t \in \R^N$: vector of realized volatilities on day $t$,
  \item $\bm{c} \in \R^N$: intercept vector,
  \item $\bm{A}_\ell \in \R^{N \times N}$: coefficient matrix at lag $\ell$,
  \item $\bm{u}_t$: reduced-form innovation vector with covariance $\bm{\Sigma}$.
\end{itemize}

The key output is the \emph{moving-average representation} obtained by inverting the
VAR:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-ma}
  \by_t = \bm{\mu} + \sum_{h=0}^{\infty} \bm{\Phi}_h \, \bm{u}_{t-h},
\end{equation}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
This rewrites the system so that today's volatility is expressed as a sum of all past
shocks, weighted by how much each shock persists over time.  The matrix $\bm{\Phi}_h$
is the ``memory kernel'': entry $(j,k)$ tells you how much a surprise in asset $k$'s
vol $h$ days ago still echoes in asset $j$'s vol today.  It is the multivariate
impulse-response function.
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
The MA representation is what lets you decompose forecast errors into contributions
from each asset.  In your project, the impulse-response matrices $\bm{\Phi}_h$
underpin the Diebold-Yilmaz spillover index that enters Layer~4 of your feature
pipeline.  The total spillover index $S^{(H)}_t$ becomes a scalar regime indicator
for LightGBM, while directional spillovers identify which assets are currently
transmitting or receiving volatility shocks.
\end{projectconnection}

where the $N \times N$ matrices $\bm{\Phi}_h$ are the impulse-response coefficients
at horizon $h$.  Entry $(\bm{\Phi}_h)_{jk}$ tells you how a unit shock to asset $k$
today affects asset $j$ at horizon $h$.

\subsection{Generalized Forecast Error Variance Decomposition}

The next step decomposes the $H$-step forecast error variance of each asset into
contributions from every shock.  The generalized variant (GFEVD), introduced by
\citet{PesaranShin1998} and adopted by \citet{DieboldYilmaz2012}, does not depend
on variable ordering (unlike Cholesky decompositions).

\begin{definition}[Generalized Forecast Error Variance Decomposition]
The fraction of asset $j$'s $H$-step forecast-error variance attributable to
shocks originating in asset $k$ is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gfevd}
  \theta_{jk}^{(H)}
  = \frac{
      \sigma_{kk}^{-1} \sum_{h=0}^{H-1}
        \bigl(\bm{e}_j' \, \bm{\Phi}_h \, \bm{\Sigma} \, \bm{e}_k \bigr)^2
    }{
      \sum_{h=0}^{H-1}
        \bm{e}_j' \, \bm{\Phi}_h \, \bm{\Sigma} \, \bm{\Phi}_h' \, \bm{e}_j
    },
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\sigma_{kk}$: the $(k,k)$ entry of $\bm{\Sigma}$ (variance of shock $k$),
  \item $\bm{e}_j$: the $j$-th column of the $N \times N$ identity matrix,
  \item $\bm{\Phi}_h$: the impulse-response matrix at horizon $h$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:var-ma},
  \item $H$: forecast horizon (typically 10 days).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
This formula answers a simple question: of all the uncertainty in asset $j$'s
volatility forecast $H$ days ahead, what fraction was caused by a shock to asset $k$?
The numerator isolates the contribution of shock $k$ (scaled by its own variance),
and the denominator is the total forecast uncertainty for asset $j$.  When $j = k$,
you get the ``own'' contribution; when $j \neq k$, you get the spillover from $k$
to $j$.
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
The GFEVD is the core building block for spillover features.  Each off-diagonal
entry $\theta_{jk}^{(H)}$ is a direct measure of cross-asset vol predictability:
it tells you how much of asset $j$'s vol surprise came from asset $k$.  These
entries feed the Diebold-Yilmaz decomposition that produces your Layer~4
spillover features for LightGBM.
\end{projectconnection}

Because GFEVD rows do not sum to one in general, normalize each row:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gfevd-norm}
  \widetilde{\theta}_{jk}^{(H)}
  = \frac{\theta_{jk}^{(H)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} \theta_{jk}^{(H)}},
  \qquad \text{so that } \sum_{k=1}^{N} \widetilde{\theta}_{jk}^{(H)} = 1.
\end{equation}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
The raw GFEVD fractions for a given asset do not necessarily add up to 100\% because
the generalized approach allows shocks to be correlated.  This normalization simply
rescales each row so that the contributions from all assets (including itself) sum to
one.  After normalization, you can read each entry as a percentage: ``$X$\% of asset
$j$'s vol forecast error is attributable to shocks from asset $k$.''
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
Normalized entries are directly interpretable as edge weights in the spillover
network.  For your feature matrix, these percentages can be used as-is: the
``directional FROM'' feature for each asset is just the sum of its off-diagonal
row entries.
\end{projectconnection}

\subsection{Spillover Measures}

From the normalized decomposition table $\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}^{(H)}$, three
spillover measures follow immediately.

\begin{keyidea}[Diebold--Yilmaz Spillover Decomposition]
\textbf{Total spillover index.}  The fraction of total forecast-error variance due
to cross-asset shocks:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total-spillover}
  S^{(H)} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\substack{j,k=1 \\ j \neq k}}^{N}
    \widetilde{\theta}_{jk}^{(H)} \times 100.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Directional FROM.}  How much volatility asset $j$ \emph{receives} from all
others:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:from-spillover}
  S_{j \leftarrow \bullet}^{(H)}
  = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \neq j}}^{N}
    \widetilde{\theta}_{jk}^{(H)} \times 100.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Directional TO.}  How much volatility asset $j$ \emph{transmits} to all
others:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:to-spillover}
  S_{j \rightarrow \bullet}^{(H)}
  = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \neq j}}^{N}
    \widetilde{\theta}_{kj}^{(H)} \times 100.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Net spillover.}  Transmitters minus receivers:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:net-spillover}
  S_j^{\text{net},(H)}
  = S_{j \rightarrow \bullet}^{(H)} - S_{j \leftarrow \bullet}^{(H)}.
\end{equation}
A positive net spillover means asset $j$ is a \emph{net transmitter} of volatility;
negative means \emph{net receiver}.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
These four measures are the feature engineering payoff of the DY framework.  Total
spillover $S^{(H)}_t$ is a regime indicator (high = crisis = different vol dynamics).
Directional FROM measures how ``vulnerable'' an asset is to imported vol.  Net
spillover identifies transmitters vs.\ receivers.  All three become columns in your
Layer~4 feature matrix, entering LightGBM as scalar cross-asset signals.  Their
value is concentrated in regime transitions---precisely the forecasts where
single-asset features alone break down.
\end{projectconnection}

The framework evolved across three papers:
\citet{DieboldYilmaz2009} introduced the total index using a Cholesky decomposition,
\citet{DieboldYilmaz2012} added directional measures and switched to GFEVD,
and \citet{DieboldYilmaz2014} refined the generalized VAR approach and applied it
to a broader set of markets.

\subsection{Worked Example: 3-Asset VAR(1)}

\begin{workedexample}[Computing Spillover Indices]
Consider three assets (Equity, Bonds, FX) with the following normalized 10-step
GFEVD table (each row sums to 100\%):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc|c}
\toprule
& \textbf{Equity} & \textbf{Bonds} & \textbf{FX}
& \textbf{FROM others} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Equity} & 60 & 25 & 15 & 40 \\
\textbf{Bonds}  & 30 & 55 & 15 & 45 \\
\textbf{FX}     & 20 & 10 & 70 & 30 \\
\midrule
\textbf{TO others} & 50 & 35 & 30 & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Step 1: Directional FROM.}  Sum the off-diagonal entries in each row,
divided by $N = 3$:
\begin{align*}
  S_{\text{Equity} \leftarrow \bullet} &= \frac{25 + 15}{3} = 13.3\%, \\
  S_{\text{Bonds} \leftarrow \bullet}  &= \frac{30 + 15}{3} = 15.0\%, \\
  S_{\text{FX} \leftarrow \bullet}     &= \frac{20 + 10}{3} = 10.0\%.
\end{align*}

\textbf{Step 2: Directional TO.}  Sum the off-diagonal entries in each column,
divided by $N$:
\begin{align*}
  S_{\text{Equity} \rightarrow \bullet} &= \frac{30 + 20}{3} = 16.7\%, \\
  S_{\text{Bonds} \rightarrow \bullet}  &= \frac{25 + 10}{3} = 11.7\%, \\
  S_{\text{FX} \rightarrow \bullet}     &= \frac{15 + 15}{3} = 10.0\%.
\end{align*}

\textbf{Step 3: Net spillover.}
\begin{align*}
  S_{\text{Equity}}^{\text{net}} &= 16.7 - 13.3 = +3.3\% \quad \text{(net transmitter)}, \\
  S_{\text{Bonds}}^{\text{net}}  &= 11.7 - 15.0 = -3.3\% \quad \text{(net receiver)}, \\
  S_{\text{FX}}^{\text{net}}     &= 10.0 - 10.0 = \phantom{+}0.0\% \quad \text{(neutral)}.
\end{align*}

\textbf{Step 4: Total spillover index.}  Sum of all off-diagonal entries,
divided by $N$ (since each row already sums to 100):
\[
  S^{(10)} = \frac{25 + 15 + 30 + 15 + 20 + 10}{3}
           = \frac{115}{3} \approx 38.3\%.
\]

Interpretation: about 38\% of the total forecast-error variance in this system comes
from cross-asset shocks rather than own shocks.  Equity is the dominant transmitter.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Time-Varying Spillovers}\label{sec:tv-spillovers}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The static spillover table in the previous section gives you a single snapshot.
In practice, connectedness changes dramatically over time: calm markets have low
spillovers, crises have high ones.  This section covers two methods to capture the
dynamics.

\subsection{Rolling-Window Approach}

The simplest method: re-estimate the VAR and GFEVD on a rolling window of
$w$ days (typically $w = 200$) and plot $S^{(H)}_t$ over time.

\begin{keyidea}[Rolling-Window Spillover]
For each day $t$, estimate the VAR on $\{t - w + 1, \ldots, t\}$, compute the GFEVD,
and record $S^{(H)}_t$.  The resulting time series reveals spillover regimes:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Calm periods: $S^{(H)}_t \approx 30$--$40\%$,
  \item Crises (2008 GFC, 2020 COVID): $S^{(H)}_t$ spikes to 70--$85\%$.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyidea}

\begin{warning}[Window-length sensitivity]
The rolling-window approach introduces a hyperparameter $w$ that affects both level
and smoothness.  Too short ($w < 100$): noisy, unstable VAR estimates.
Too long ($w > 300$): sluggish, crises get averaged out.  Always report results for
at least two window lengths to check robustness \citep{DieboldYilmaz2012}.
\end{warning}

\subsection{TVP-VAR Connectedness}

\citet{AntonakakisChatziantoniouGabauer2020} replace the rolling-window VAR with a
\emph{time-varying parameter VAR} (TVP-VAR) estimated via the Kalman filter.  This
eliminates the window-length hyperparameter entirely.

The TVP-VAR model allows coefficients to drift:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tvp-var}
  \by_t = \bm{c}_t + \sum_{\ell=1}^{p} \bm{A}_{\ell,t} \, \by_{t-\ell}
          + \bm{u}_t,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}_t) = \operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}_{t-1}) + \bm{\eta}_t,
\end{equation}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
This is the same VAR as before, but now the coefficient matrices $\bm{A}_{\ell,t}$
are allowed to change every day.  The second equation says that today's coefficients
equal yesterday's plus a small random drift $\bm{\eta}_t$.  The Kalman filter
estimates the evolving coefficients without needing a rolling window, so the spillover
index updates smoothly each day rather than jumping when observations enter or leave
a fixed window.
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
TVP-VAR connectedness responds faster to regime changes than rolling-window estimates,
making it a better real-time feature for your ML model.  When the connectedness index
spikes, your model knows that vol dynamics have shifted to ``crisis mode'' with higher
persistence and stronger cross-asset effects.  This is a natural conditioning variable
for the HAR baseline: interact HAR lags with a high-connectedness indicator to let the
model adapt its decay structure in real time.
\end{projectconnection}

where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\bm{A}_{\ell,t}$: time-varying coefficient matrix at lag $\ell$ and time $t$,
  \item $\bm{\eta}_t \sim (0, \bm{Q})$: state innovation with covariance $\bm{Q}$
        governing the speed of parameter drift.
\end{itemize}

At each $t$, the Kalman filter produces updated coefficient estimates, and you
compute the GFEVD exactly as before to get $S^{(H)}_t$.

\begin{keyresult}[TVP-VAR vs.\ Rolling Window]
\citet{AntonakakisChatziantoniouGabauer2020} show that TVP-VAR connectedness is
smoother than rolling-window estimates, avoids the abrupt ``entry/exit'' artifacts
when extreme observations enter or leave the window, and responds faster to genuine
structural breaks.  Both methods agree on the broad pattern: spillovers spike during
crises and monetary-policy surprises, but the TVP-VAR resolves timing more sharply.
\end{keyresult}

\section{Network Visualization and Interpretation}\label{sec:network-viz}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The GFEVD table is a matrix of numbers.  Networks make that matrix readable at a
glance.  This section covers the visual conventions and the patterns you should look
for.

\subsection{Visual Encoding}

Four encoding rules produce an interpretable graph:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Node size} proportional to total connectedness
        ($S_{j \rightarrow \bullet} + S_{j \leftarrow \bullet}$).
        Large nodes are ``systemically important'' regardless of sign.
  \item \textbf{Edge thickness} proportional to pairwise spillover
        $\widetilde{\theta}_{jk}^{(H)}$.  Only draw edges above a threshold
        (e.g., 5\%) to avoid clutter.
  \item \textbf{Edge direction} from shock source $k$ to shock recipient $j$
        (arrow points toward the asset whose variance is explained).
  \item \textbf{Node color} by net spillover sign: red for net transmitters,
        blue for net receivers.  Alternatively, color by sector or asset class.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Calm vs.\ Crisis Networks}

The most striking pattern in spillover networks is the structural shift between
tranquil and stressed markets \citep{DemirerDieboldLiuYilmaz2018}.

Three stylized facts from the DY network literature:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]

        (30--40\%).
  \item \textbf{Crises}: cross-sector connectedness surges.  The network topology
        shifts from clustered to nearly fully connected.  Total spillover jumps to
        70--85\%.
  \item \textbf{Net transmitter identity shifts}: in calm markets, commodity shocks
        are often isolated; during crises, equity and credit become dominant
        transmitters \citep{DieboldYilmaz2014}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyidea}

\section{Cross-Asset Universality}\label{sec:universality}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The high cross-sector connectedness during crises hints at something deeper: maybe
volatility dynamics are not just correlated but fundamentally \emph{similar} across
asset classes.  Two recent papers make this case precisely.

\citet{SirignanoCont2019} train a single deep network (LSTM layers followed by a
fully-connected layer) to predict the direction of the next price move by pooling
high-frequency data across hundreds of US equities.
The pooled (``universal'') model outperforms individual asset-specific
models, implying that the features driving price formation are largely shared.

\begin{keyresult}[Universal Price Formation Features]
\citet{SirignanoCont2019} show that a model trained on pooled equity data generalizes
to out-of-sample stocks not seen during training.  This ``universality'' holds for
features based on order-flow history (queue imbalances, trade arrivals).  The implication:
spillover networks may reflect common factor exposures rather than direct causal
transmission.
\end{keyresult}

\citet{RosenbaumZhang2022} push universality further by training a universal LSTM
on pooled data from hundreds of liquid stocks to forecast daily realized volatility.
Their key finding: the LSTM's predictions are matched by a parsimonious rough
volatility model with $H \approx 0.1$, consistent with the rough volatility theory
from Chapter~\ref{ch:rough}.

\begin{intuition}[Why universality matters for spillovers]
If volatility dynamics really are ``universal'' (same roughness, same feature
importance across assets), then the strong cross-asset connectedness you see during
crises may not require a contagion story.  Instead, a single common factor (e.g.,
dealer risk capacity, funding liquidity) could drive all assets simultaneously.
This does not diminish the usefulness of spillover indices as diagnostic tools,
but it changes how you interpret them: high spillover may reflect common-factor
exposure rather than sequential transmission from one asset to the next.
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
Universality has a direct architectural implication for your project.  If vol dynamics
are truly shared across assets, you can train a single model on pooled data (all
assets stacked) rather than fitting $N$ separate models.  This dramatically increases
your effective sample size and reduces overfitting.  Even for a univariate HAR-based
forecast, pooling across assets with shared features (same lags, same jump indicators)
can improve out-of-sample QLIKE.
\end{projectconnection}


\subsection{Pooled Panel Forecasting Across Instruments}\label{sec:universality-panel}

Pooling raises a concrete question the box above left open:
how do you feed RV histories for all $34$ instruments in your project into a single
HAR regression or a single LightGBM, without (a) drowning the few liquid index
futures under the $30$ mega-cap equities, (b) letting the model confuse a
structurally high-vol name with a structurally low-vol one, or (c) leaking
information across instruments on a crisis day?  This subsection answers those
questions for the two model classes you actually use as baselines --- pooled
linear/HAR and pooled trees.  It stays on forecasting; it does not wander into
causal questions like ``did asset A's vol \emph{cause} asset B's'' --- that is a
separate topic.

\begin{prereq}[Panel Data]
Until now this guide has treated each instrument as its own time series: one
column of $\RV_t$, fit a HAR, repeat $34$ times.  A \textbf{panel} (or
\textbf{longitudinal}) data set instead tracks multiple \textbf{entities}
$i = 1, \ldots, N$ over multiple time periods $t = 1, \ldots, T$, with each
observation indexed by the pair $(i,t)$.  Here the entities are the $N = 34$
instruments (the $30$ mega-cap equities plus $4$ sector/index ETFs and the
E-mini S\&P~500 future from Chapter~\ref{ch:features}), the time index $t$ runs
over trading days, and the
target $y_{it}$ is next-day (or $h$-day) realized volatility for instrument $i$.
When every instrument is observed on every date the panel is \textbf{balanced};
when some $(i,t)$ cells are missing it is \textbf{unbalanced} (treated at the end
of this subsection).
\end{prereq}

Fitting $34$ separate HARs throws away an obvious resource: the instruments share
dynamics.  Chapter~\ref{ch:rough} and the universality results above
\citep{SirignanoCont2019,RosenbaumZhang2022} argue that the \emph{shape} of
volatility dynamics --- HAR decay structure, jump sensitivity, the leverage tilt
--- is broadly common across liquid assets.  If that is even approximately true,
then stacking the $34$ instruments into one $(i,t)$ panel lets a single set of
coefficients borrow strength from every series at once.

\begin{intuition}[Why stacking grows the sample]
A single instrument with five years of daily data gives you $T \approx 1{,}250$
rows --- a thin sample for a flexible model.  Stack $N = 34$ instruments and the
pooled panel has up to $N \times T \approx 42{,}500$ rows.  You have not invented
new information about any one stock, but you \emph{have} given the model $34$
independent realizations of the same volatility-formation process to estimate the
\emph{shared} parameters from.  That is exactly the lever a univariate HAR cannot
pull.
\end{intuition}

\subsubsection*{A pooled HAR baseline, and why naive pooling fails}

The naive move is to stack every instrument's HAR \textbf{design matrix} --- just
the table of feature values, one row per $(\text{instrument},\text{day})$ and one
column per feature --- and run one OLS (\textbf{ordinary least squares}, the
standard best-fit-line procedure).  Stacking means literally laying each
instrument's feature table on top of the next into one tall table.  The problem is
\textbf{instrument-specific heterogeneity}: a high-beta growth name
lives at a structurally higher average $\RV$ than a defensive utility, and a
single common \textbf{intercept} (the baseline level the line starts from) cannot
represent both.  Forcing one intercept makes the
pooled fit chase the cross-instrument \emph{level} differences instead of the
volatility \emph{dynamics} you actually want to learn.  The fix is an
\textbf{entity fixed effect}: one intercept per instrument.  Because each
instrument carries many features rather than one, the single slope $m$ of a
straight line $y=mx+b$ becomes a \textbf{slope vector} $\bbeta$ with one slope per
feature.

The entity fixed-effects model gives each instrument its own
baseline volatility level while sharing one common slope vector across all of
them:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:panel-fe}
  y_{it} = \underbrace{\alpha_i}_{\text{instrument level}}
           + \bx_{it}'\bbeta + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
The notation $\bx_{it}'\bbeta$ (read ``$x$-transpose-$\bbeta$''; the raised tick
mark $'$ is the \emph{transpose}, which here just turns the column of features into
a row so the next step is a sum) is shorthand for multiplying each of the $K$
features by its matching coefficient in $\bbeta$ and adding them into a single
predicted number --- the same $\beta_1\,\text{feature}_1 + \beta_2\,\text{feature}_2
+ \cdots$ you saw in the HAR regression of Chapter~\ref{ch:features}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $y_{it}$: the forecast target for instrument $i$ on day $t$ (next-day
        $\RV_{i,t+1}$, or a log/$\sqrt{}$ transform of it as in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:features}),
  \item $\alpha_i$: the \emph{instrument-specific intercept}, absorbing every
        time-invariant difference across instruments (a name's average vol level,
        its sector's baseline turbulence),
  \item $\bx_{it}$: the $K\times1$ vector (a single column of the $K$ HAR-type
        features, where $K$ is simply however many predictors you stacked) for
        instrument $i$ on day $t$ --- the same HAR-type features defined in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:features}: daily, weekly, and monthly lagged $\RV$, the
        jump and semivariance splits, $\VRP$, and so on,
  \item $\bbeta$: the $K\times1$ slope vector, \emph{common across all
        instruments} (one HAR decay structure for the whole panel),
  \item $\varepsilon_{it}$: the idiosyncratic error --- the leftover the model
        cannot explain --- assumed to average to zero,
        $\E[\varepsilon_{it}\mid\alpha_i,\bx_{it}] = 0$.  That statement reads:
        averaging ($\E[\cdot]$) over everything, given ($\mid$) the instrument and
        its feature values, the leftover error is zero no matter which instrument
        we look at or what its features are --- i.e.\ the model is unbiased.
\end{itemize}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
Equation~\eqref{eq:panel-fe} says: ``Let every instrument sit at its own resting
volatility level $\alpha_i$, but make them all obey the \emph{same} rule for how
yesterday's, last week's, and last month's vol map into tomorrow's.''  The
fixed effect quarantines the boring, permanent level differences into $\alpha_i$
so that the interesting, shared dynamics live entirely in $\bbeta$.  You estimate
one HAR for the whole desk instead of $34$ noisy ones.
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
This is the pooled-HAR baseline your project should beat before reaching for
trees or nets.  It is the panel analogue of the single-instrument HAR of
\citet{Corsi2009}: identical features, but the slope $\bbeta$ is now estimated
from $34\times$ the data, which sharply tightens the $\QLIKE$ (the volatility
forecast-accuracy score from Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}; lower is better) on
short-history names that could never support a stable HAR on their own.  Report its
out-of-sample $\QLIKE$ against the per-instrument HARs; the pooled fit usually wins on the
thin-history instruments and roughly ties on the data-rich index lines.
\end{projectconnection}

You do not have to literally add $34$ dummy columns.  The algebraically
identical \textbf{within estimator} subtracts each instrument's own time-series
mean from both sides, cancelling $\alpha_i$.

Demeaning each instrument's series removes its fixed effect,
leaving a clean regression for $\bbeta$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:panel-within}
  \underbrace{(y_{it} - \bar{y}_i)}_{\text{demeaned target}}
  = (\bx_{it} - \bar{\bx}_i)'\bbeta + (\varepsilon_{it} - \bar{\varepsilon}_i),
  \qquad
  \bar{y}_i = \frac{1}{T_i}\sum_{t} y_{it},
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\bar{y}_i,\ \bar{\bx}_i$ (read ``$y$-bar-$i$'', ``$x$-bar-$i$''): the
        time-series \emph{averages} of the target and the features \emph{within
        instrument $i$} --- simply instrument $i$'s own average vol and average
        features over its $T_i$ observed days, where $T_i$ is the number of trading
        days instrument $i$ is observed,
  \item the entity effect $\alpha_i$ cancels because it is constant over $t$ for
        each $i$, so demeaning subtracts it from itself,
  \item running OLS (the standard best-fit-line procedure) on the demeaned data
        yields what panel-data texts call the \textbf{within} estimate of $\bbeta$
        --- so named because it uses only the variation \emph{within} each
        instrument over time \citep{Wooldridge2010}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
Demeaning re-expresses every variable as a \emph{deviation from that
instrument's own average}.  Instead of asking ``is this stock's vol high?'' (a
level question contaminated by which stock it is), the regression now asks ``is
this stock's vol high \emph{relative to its own normal}, given that its lagged
vol is high relative to its own normal?''  That deviation-based question has the
same answer for every instrument, which is exactly why one shared $\bbeta$ is
legitimate.
\end{intuition}

\begin{workedexample}[Demeaning two instruments by hand]
Two instruments, three days each, raw $\RV$ (annualized \%).  Subtract each
instrument's own average ($\bar{y}_i$) from every value to get the demeaned
target $y_{it}-\bar{y}_i$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Instrument} & & \textbf{Day 1} & \textbf{Day 2} & \textbf{Day 3} \\
\midrule
High-vol name $A$ & raw $\RV$          & 40 & 50 & 45 \\
($\bar{y}_A = 45$) & demeaned          & $-5$ & $+5$ & $0$ \\[4pt]
Low-vol name $B$  & raw $\RV$          & 12 & 18 & 15 \\
($\bar{y}_B = 15$) & demeaned          & $-3$ & $+3$ & $0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The raw rows live at completely different levels ($45$ vs.\ $15$), but after
demeaning both sit around zero and only the day-to-day \emph{deviations} remain.
The structural level gap has vanished, so a single shared $\bbeta$ can now learn
the common dynamics from $A$ and $B$ on equal footing.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
Demeaning is the panel version of the per-instrument $z$-scoring already in the
Chapter~\ref{ch:features} pipeline (subtracting each instrument's mean and
dividing by its standard deviation so all names sit on a comparable scale): if
your features are already $z$-scored within instrument, the entity fixed effect is
largely redundant for the \emph{features} --- but keep it (or a demeaned target)
so the \emph{intercept} does not chase level differences.
\end{projectconnection}

\begin{warning}[Within estimation discards cross-instrument level information]
The within estimator uses only \emph{within-instrument} variation over time; it
throws away the \emph{between-instrument} variation in average levels entirely.
That is deliberate --- the level differences are the heterogeneity you wanted to
control for --- but it has a consequence: you cannot identify the effect of any
\emph{instrument-constant} feature (sector label, ``is-an-index'' flag) inside a
fixed-effects regression, because such a feature has zero within variation and
is indistinguishable from $\alpha_i$ --- the instrument's intercept already
captures anything that never changes for that instrument, so a sector label adds
no new information the regression can separate out.  If you need those level
effects, carry them as
separate dummy features in the tree model below rather than the FE regression.
\end{warning}

\subsubsection*{Time fixed effects: absorbing market-wide crisis days}

Section~\ref{sec:tv-spillovers} showed that on crisis days --- Lehman, the euro
crisis, the March~2020 COVID selloff --- \emph{every} instrument's vol spikes at
once.  In a pooled panel those common-shock days act like $34$ correlated
duplicate observations of the same event, and they can dominate the fit.  A
\textbf{time fixed effect} $\delta_t$ absorbs whatever is common to all
instruments on a given day.

Adding a per-day intercept on top of the per-instrument
intercept strips out market-wide volatility shocks:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:panel-twoway-fe}
  y_{it} = \alpha_i
           + \underbrace{\delta_t}_{\text{market-wide day shock}}
           + \bx_{it}'\bbeta + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\delta_t$: the \emph{time-specific intercept}, common to all $N$
        instruments on day $t$, absorbing aggregate vol spikes, macro releases,
        and the contagion days quantified by the total-spillover index $S^{(H)}_t$
        from Section~\ref{sec:dy-spillover} (a single number near $30$--$40\%$ in
        calm markets and $70$--$85\%$ in crises that tracks how much of the market
        is moving together),
  \item all other symbols are as in Equation~\eqref{eq:panel-fe}; with both
        $\alpha_i$ and $\delta_t$ present this is a \textbf{two-way fixed-effects}
        model.
\end{itemize}

\begin{intuition}[In Plain English]
The entity effect $\alpha_i$ asks ``which instrument is this?'' and removes it.
The time effect $\delta_t$ asks ``what was the whole market doing today?'' and
removes that too.  What survives is the \emph{relative} signal: on a day when
market vol is elevated, which names are even \emph{more} elevated than the
average, and is that excess predictable from their own lagged-vol features?
$\delta_t$ is, in effect, the regression-coefficient cousin of the
total-spillover regime indicator $S^{(H)}_t$ --- both soak up the shared crisis
component so the model can focus on cross-sectional differences.
\end{intuition}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
There is a real trade-off here for an RV forecaster.  If your deliverable is the
\emph{absolute} level of each instrument's vol (the input to a vol-targeting
strategy --- one that scales position size up when forecast vol is low and down
when it is high, so it needs the absolute level), do \emph{not} include time
effects: $\delta_t$ absorbs the common vol signal that is precisely what such a
strategy needs to size positions.  Include time effects only when you care about
the \emph{cross-sectional} question --- which names will be relatively more
volatile --- e.g.\ for a dispersion or relative-value trade, which only bets on
which names are \emph{more} volatile than others and so needs the cross-sectional
ranking rather than the absolute level.  A practical compromise that
keeps the absolute signal is to drop $\delta_t$ but add the
Section~\ref{sec:spillover-features} spillover features as columns, letting the
model condition on the regime rather than differencing it away.
\end{projectconnection}

\subsubsection*{Encoding instrument identity for a pooled LightGBM}

A tree model does not demean.  To pool $34$ instruments in LightGBM you instead
give the tree the instrument identity \emph{as a feature} and let it split on it.
With only $N = 34$ instruments, identity is \textbf{low-cardinality} --- there are
only $34$ distinct values, a small number of categories.  You encode it with
\textbf{one-hot dummy} columns: one yes/no ($1$/$0$) column per instrument that
equals $1$ only on that instrument's rows (drop one to avoid redundancy; see the
\texttt{is\_AAPL} example below).  These are cheap and LightGBM handles them
natively \citep{Ke2017}.

\begin{keyidea}[Instrument dummies recover instrument-specific dynamics for free]
When the tree splits on \texttt{is\_AAPL = 1} and then splits on the weekly-$\RV$
feature beneath that branch, it has effectively estimated a \emph{different} HAR
slope for that instrument --- a feature$\times$instrument \emph{interaction} (a
rule that lets the slope on a feature differ by instrument), and via a first split
on identity alone it also reproduces the per-instrument level $\alpha_i$ --- which
the single shared $\bbeta$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:panel-fe} can only get through
explicit interaction terms.  Pooling in a tree therefore gives you the
sample-size benefit of the panel \emph{and} per-instrument flexibility, without
fitting $34$ separate models.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{warning}[Never target-encode the instrument ID --- it leaks the future]
The tempting shortcut is \textbf{target encoding}: replace the instrument ID with
that instrument's mean target (its average $\RV$).  In a panel time series this
is a temporal-leakage trap.  The instrument mean computed over the \emph{whole}
sample includes future days, so encoding instrument $i$ on day $t$ with a mean
that already ``knows'' $\RV_{i,t+5}$ smuggles the answer into the feature.  The
damage is silent: cross-validation scores look excellent and live performance
collapses.  With only $34$ low-cardinality instruments there is \emph{no reason}
to target-encode --- plain one-hot dummies carry the same information with zero
leakage.  Reserve any encoding-by-statistic for genuinely high-cardinality
categoricals, and even then compute the statistic only on data strictly prior to
$t$.
\end{warning}

\begin{projectconnection}[Why This Matters]
This leakage is the cross-sectional cousin of the standard look-ahead bias from
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.  The same panel structure that grows your sample
also multiplies the ways the future can leak into the past, and the most
dangerous one is at the \emph{fold} level: a random $K$-fold split scatters rows
from the same date across train and test, so the model sees other instruments'
day-$t$ behaviour while predicting instrument $i$ on day $t$.  Use the
time-blocked purged CV of Section~\ref{sec:eval-purgedcv}, and follow the
cross-sectional leakage rule in \Cref{sec:ch16:xsection-leakage}: entire
\emph{dates} go to train or test as a block, never split across folds.
\end{projectconnection}

\subsubsection*{Unbalanced panels and short-history instruments}

Your panel will almost certainly be \textbf{unbalanced}: a name added to the
index two years ago has half the history of the index future, and a recent IPO
has a few hundred days at most.  Both the within estimator and a tree handle this
without special pleading --- demeaning in Equation~\eqref{eq:panel-within} uses
whatever $T_i$ days exist for instrument $i$, and a tree simply sees fewer rows
for that name --- but two cautions apply specifically to RV forecasting.

\begin{warning}[Short histories distort a pooled fit two ways]
First, a short-history instrument whose entire window happens to sit inside a
calm (or a crisis) regime contributes a biased view of the shared dynamics;
check that your pooled $\bbeta$ and $\QLIKE$ are not driven by the longest series
alone by re-fitting on the balanced sub-panel of full-history instruments.
Second, in a \emph{dynamic} pooled panel where the target depends on its own lag
(as HAR does), demeaning a very short series induces a small finite-sample bias
that shrinks as the history $T_i$ grows \citep{Nickell1981}.  Intuitively, because
the lagged target is part of what you averaged away, the demeaned lag and the
demeaned error end up slightly linked, nudging the estimated persistence a touch
too low --- the Nickell bias.  At $T_i \approx 1{,}000$ daily
observations this bias is negligible, but it becomes material if you aggregate to
weekly or monthly targets on a name with only a year or two of data.
\end{warning}

The practical recipe for the pooled RV panel:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Stack the $34$ instruments into an $(i,t)$ panel; $z$-score every feature
        \emph{within instrument} using a trailing window
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:features}).
  \item For the linear baseline, use entity fixed effects
        (Equation~\eqref{eq:panel-fe}); add time effects only if the target is
        cross-sectional rather than absolute.
  \item For the tree, add $N-1$ one-hot instrument dummies; \emph{never}
        target-encode the ID.
  \item Validate with date-blocked purged CV (Section~\ref{sec:eval-purgedcv};
        \Cref{sec:ch16:xsection-leakage}), so whole dates, not random
        rows, define the folds.
  \item Sanity-check robustness by re-fitting on the balanced full-history
        sub-panel; report $\QLIKE$ for both.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Spillover Indices as Predictive Features}\label{sec:spillover-features}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The previous sections treated spillover indices as descriptive diagnostics.
This section flips the lens: can you use them as \emph{inputs} to the forecasting
models from Chapters~\ref{ch:trees}--\ref{ch:hybrid}?  The answer is a qualified
yes, with important caveats.

\subsection{Three Feature Families}

\begin{keyidea}[Spillover-Based Features for ML]
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Total spillover as regime indicator.}
        High $S^{(H)}_t$ signals ``crisis mode'' where correlations are elevated and
        volatility persistence is stronger.  Use it as a conditioning variable: for
        example, a LightGBM model can split on $S^{(H)}_t > 60$ to activate
        crisis-specific trees.

  \item \textbf{Directional FROM as vulnerability measure.}
        An asset with a high and rising $S_{j \leftarrow \bullet}$ is absorbing
        shocks from many sources.  This predicts higher future volatility for asset
        $j$, especially in the 5--20 day horizon.  See Chapter~\ref{ch:features}
        for how to z-score and lag these features.

  \item \textbf{Net spillover as contrarian signal.}
        Extreme net receivers ($S_j^{\text{net}} \ll 0$) tend to mean-revert:
        assets that have absorbed large cross-market shocks often see volatility
        revert to lower levels once the contagion subsides.  This generates a
        medium-horizon (1--3 month) signal for volatility forecasting.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Practical Considerations}

\begin{warning}[Spillover features are slow-moving]
Spillover indices computed from a 200-day rolling window update slowly.  For
short-horizon forecasts (1--5 days), they may add little predictive power beyond
the VIX or a simple HAR model.  Their value is highest for medium-to-long horizons
(1 week to 3 months) and for regime-conditional models.

Additionally, the TVP-VAR variant from \citet{AntonakakisChatziantoniouGabauer2020}
responds faster than rolling-window estimates, making it more suitable as an ML
input if you need timelier signals.
\end{warning}

A practical recipe for incorporating spillover features:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Compute the DY spillover index and directional measures using a 200-day
        rolling window (or TVP-VAR).
  \item Construct three features per asset: total spillover $S^{(H)}_t$, directional
        FROM $S_{j \leftarrow \bullet}^{(H)}$, and net spillover
        $S_j^{\text{net},(H)}$.
  \item Z-score each feature using a 252-day trailing window.
  \item Include as additional columns in the feature matrix from
        Chapter~\ref{ch:features}, alongside HAR lags, VRP, and other predictors.
  \item Check $\SHAP$ importance: if spillover features rank below the top 10 in a
        LightGBM model, they likely add noise rather than signal for your target
        horizon.
\end{enumerate}


% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Summary}\label{sec:spillovers-summary}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The Diebold--Yilmaz framework measures volatility spillovers using a VAR
        on realized volatilities and generalized forecast error variance
        decomposition (GFEVD).

  \item The \textbf{total spillover index} $S^{(H)}$ captures the fraction of
        forecast-error variance explained by cross-asset shocks; typical values are
        30--40\% in calm markets, 70--85\% during crises.

  \item \textbf{Directional spillovers} (FROM, TO, net) identify which assets
        transmit and which receive volatility shocks; equity and credit tend to be
        net transmitters during stress.

  \item Rolling-window estimation (200-day window) is simple but introduces a
        window-length hyperparameter; the TVP-VAR approach of
        \citet{AntonakakisChatziantoniouGabauer2020} eliminates this choice.

  \item Network visualization reveals that calm-period connectedness is within-sector
        (clustered), while crisis-period connectedness is cross-sector (dense, nearly
        fully connected).

  \item \citet{DemirerDieboldLiuYilmaz2018} applied the DY framework to a global
        network of banks and sovereigns, confirming the calm-to-crisis structural
        shift at institution level.

  \item \citet{SirignanoCont2019} demonstrate ``universal'' price formation features:
        a pooled deep learning model trained across US equities outperforms
        asset-specific models, suggesting common underlying dynamics.

  \item \citet{RosenbaumZhang2022} train a universal LSTM on pooled equity data
        whose predictions are matched by a rough volatility model with
        $H \approx 0.1$, connecting spillover phenomena to rough volatility
        theory (Chapter~\ref{ch:rough}).

  \item Spillover indices are useful as ML features for medium-horizon forecasting:
        total spillover as a regime indicator, directional FROM as a vulnerability
        measure, and net spillover as a contrarian signal.

  \item Spillover features are slow-moving (200-day window) and may add limited value
        for short-horizon forecasts; always verify with $\SHAP$ importance analysis.

  \item The GFEVD approach generalizes the Cholesky decomposition and does not depend
        on variable ordering, making it suitable for large cross-sections.

  \item High cross-asset spillovers during crises may reflect common-factor exposure
        (funding liquidity, dealer balance sheets) rather than sequential causal
        contagion.
\end{itemize}
