\section{Why This Chapter Matters}

\begin{application}[From Components to Combinations]
Chapters~\ref{ch:trees} and~\ref{ch:deeplearning} showed that ML models
improve on HAR primarily through richer features and nonlinear interactions,
not by replacing the linear structure that HAR captures well.  This chapter
asks: why not let HAR handle the linear part and train ML only on the
residual?  Hybrid models do this, and they are the safest bet in the
volatility forecasting literature~\citep{RahimikiaPoon2020}.  Project~1
(HARQ-X with ML Residual Augmentation) is a hybrid by design.
\end{application}

You already have two powerful toolkits: the econometric models of
Chapters~\ref{ch:garch}--\ref{ch:har} and the machine-learning models of
Chapters~\ref{ch:trees}--\ref{ch:deeplearning}.  Each has blind spots.
HAR is parsimonious but linear; gradient-boosted trees are flexible but
hungry for signal.  This chapter shows you how to combine them so that
each component operates where it is strongest.

The core philosophy is simple: \emph{decompose}, then \emph{specialize}.
Let a well-understood econometric model absorb the predictable structure,
then aim the full power of ML at whatever remains.  The resulting hybrid
almost always outperforms either component alone, and it does so with
lower variance and greater interpretability than a pure ML
approach~\citep{RahimikiaPoon2020}.

\section{Why Hybrids Win}\label{sec:hybrid:why}

\subsection{The Variance Budget Argument}

Consider a forecast target $\RV_{t+1}$.  A fitted HAR model typically
explains 40--60\% of next-day realized-volatility variation with just three
coefficients~\citep{RahimikiaPoon2020}.  That leaves 40--60\% in the
residual $e_t = \RV_{t+1} - \widehat{\HAR}_t$.  If you train an ML model
directly on $\RV_{t+1}$, it must rediscover the linear structure that HAR
already captures perfectly, wasting model capacity and inviting overfitting.
If instead you train on $e_t$, three things change for the better:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{HAR never overfits.}  Three coefficients on lagged
        averages cannot memorize noise, so the linear component is
        rock-solid out of sample.
  \item \textbf{Residual targets have lower variance.}  The signal-to-noise
        ratio for the ML component improves because you have removed the
        dominant low-frequency trend.
  \item \textbf{Ensemble diversification.}  Even if the ML model adds only
        modest accuracy, combining two weakly correlated forecasters reduces
        overall forecast variance by the standard diversification
        identity~\citep{RahimikiaPoon2020}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keyidea}[Let HAR Do the Heavy Lifting]
If HAR explains 60\% of next-day $\RV$ with 3 coefficients, let it.
Train ML on the residual.  You get the reliability of HAR plus the
flexibility of ML, without asking either model to do the other's job.
\end{keyidea}

never sees the raw target; it only sees the residual that HAR could not

\section{HAR-SVR: Support Vector Regression on HAR Residuals}
\label{sec:hybrid:har-svr}

\subsection{Intuition}

Support vector regression (SVR) is a natural first choice for the residual
stage because its $\varepsilon$-insensitive loss function automatically
ignores small residuals.  If the HAR fit is already good for most days,
many residuals will be near zero.  SVR treats those as ``correct enough''
and focuses capacity on the days where HAR fails most, such as
post-jump or regime-change dates~\citep{RahimikiaPoon2020}.

\begin{intuition}[Why SVR Suits Residuals]
SVR's $\varepsilon$-tube acts as a built-in noise filter.  Days where HAR
is close enough (residual inside the tube) contribute zero loss.  SVR
concentrates its support vectors on the hard cases, which is exactly what
you want when most of the signal has already been removed.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Procedure}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fit HAR on the training set:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:har-fit-hybrid}
          \widehat{\HAR}_t = \hat{\beta}_0
            + \hat{\beta}_d\,\RV_{t}
            + \hat{\beta}_w\,\RV_{t}^{(w)}
            + \hat{\beta}_m\,\RV_{t}^{(m)}.
        \end{equation}
  \item Compute training residuals:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:har-resid}
          e_t = \RV_{t+1} - \widehat{\HAR}_t.
        \end{equation}
  \item Construct a feature matrix $\bX_t$ from the feature engineering
        toolkit of Chapter~\ref{ch:features} (jump indicators, leverage,
        signed volatility, VIX basis, microstructure noise proxies).
  \item Fit SVR with radial basis function (RBF) kernel on $(e_t, \bX_t)$:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:svr-loss}
          \min_{w,b}\;\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2
            + C\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\!\bigl(0,\,|e_t - f(\bX_t)| - \varepsilon\bigr),
        \end{equation}
        where each term is:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item $\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2$ --- the regularization penalty that
                controls model complexity,
          \item $C$ --- the trade-off parameter between margin width and
                training error,
          \item $\varepsilon$ --- the tube width; residuals smaller than
                $\varepsilon$ in absolute value incur zero loss,
          \item $f(\bX_t) = w^\top \phi(\bX_t) + b$ --- the SVR prediction
                in the kernel-induced feature space.
        \end{itemize}
  \item Produce the combined forecast:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:har-svr-combined}
          \hat{y}_{t+1} = \widehat{\HAR}_t + \widehat{\text{SVR}}(\bX_t).
        \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{warning}[Tune $\varepsilon$ Carefully]
Setting $\varepsilon$ too large makes the SVR ignore all residuals.
Setting it too small turns SVR into standard squared-loss regression and
removes the noise-filtering benefit.  Cross-validate $\varepsilon$ on
$\QLIKE$ or MSE, not on the number of support vectors.
\end{warning}

\section{GARCH-Informed Neural Networks}\label{sec:hybrid:ginn}

\subsection{Motivation}

The models in Sections~\ref{sec:hybrid:why}--\ref{sec:hybrid:har-svr} use
a two-stage pipeline: fit an econometric model, then correct its errors.
GARCH-Informed Neural Networks (GINN) take a different approach.
They hard-wire the GARCH recursion directly into the neural network
architecture so that the network learns corrections to the GARCH
parameters rather than learning the entire volatility dynamics from
scratch~\citep{GINN2024}.

\begin{keyidea}[GINN = Residual Learning for GARCH]
GINN is to GARCH what a residual network is to a feedforward net.
Instead of asking the network to learn $\sigma^2_{t+1}$ from raw data,
you give it the GARCH update equation as a scaffold and let the network
learn only the deviations.  This dramatically reduces the function space
the network must search.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Architecture}

The standard GARCH(1,1) update is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:garch-update}
  \sigma^2_{t+1} = \omega + \alpha\,\epsilon_t^2 + \beta\,\sigma^2_t.
\end{equation}

GINN replaces the fixed parameters $(\omega, \alpha, \beta)$ with
time-varying outputs of a neural network $g_\btheta$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ginn}
  \sigma^2_{t+1} = \omega_t + \alpha_t\,\epsilon_t^2
    + \beta_t\,\sigma^2_t,
  \qquad
  (\omega_t, \alpha_t, \beta_t) = g_\btheta(\bX_t),
\end{equation}
where each term is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $g_\btheta(\bX_t)$ --- a small feedforward network (typically 2
        hidden layers, 32--64 units each) that maps auxiliary features
        $\bX_t$ to time-varying GARCH parameters,
  \item $\omega_t, \alpha_t, \beta_t$ --- the GARCH coefficients at time
        $t$, constrained to satisfy $\omega_t > 0$, $\alpha_t \geq 0$,
        $\beta_t \geq 0$, and $\alpha_t + \beta_t < 1$ via softmax and
        sigmoid output activations~\citep{GINN2024},
  \item $\epsilon_t^2$ --- the squared innovation (return shock),
  \item $\sigma^2_t$ --- the previous conditional variance, fed back
        recurrently.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why This Works}

The key advantage is that GINN preserves the inductive bias of GARCH:
volatility clusters, shocks decay exponentially, and the unconditional
variance is finite.  The neural network modulates these dynamics based on
auxiliary information (sentiment, jump indicators, cross-asset signals)
without having to discover the clustering structure itself.
\citet{GINN2024} show that GINN matches or outperforms both standard
GARCH and unconstrained LSTMs on equity index volatility, with
substantially fewer parameters than the LSTM.

\section{NLP-Augmented Volatility Models}\label{sec:hybrid:nlp}

\subsection{Motivation}

News moves volatility, and the effect is asymmetric: negative news
amplifies $\RV$ far more than positive news calms it.
\citet{RahimikiaZohrenPoon2021NLP} ask whether a machine-readable
news signal can improve HAR forecasts.  The answer is yes, but modestly
and conditionally.

\subsection{The Rahimikia-Zohren-Poon Pipeline}

The procedure has three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Text embedding.}  Collect financial news articles
        (e.g., from Reuters or Bloomberg) for each trading day.  Train
        or load a Word2Vec model on a financial corpus.  Represent each
        article as the average of its word vectors, then aggregate to a
        daily sentiment score $s_t$ via a shallow classifier
        trained on labeled sentiment data~\citep{RahimikiaZohrenPoon2021NLP}.
  \item \textbf{Augmented HAR.}  Append the sentiment score to the
        standard HAR specification:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:har-nlp}
          \RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d\,\RV_t
            + \beta_w\,\RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\,\RV_t^{(m)}
            + \gamma\,s_t + \varepsilon_{t+1},
        \end{equation}
        where:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item $s_t$ --- the daily sentiment score (negative values
                indicate bearish tone),
          \item $\gamma$ --- the sentiment loading; if news carries
                incremental information beyond lagged $\RV$, then
                $\gamma \neq 0$.
        \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Evaluation.}  Compare $\QLIKE$ loss of HAR vs.\
        HAR$+$NLP across the full sample and conditional on high-volatility
        periods.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What the Literature Finds}

\citet{RahimikiaZohrenPoon2021NLP} report 1--3\% improvement in $\QLIKE$
on average, with gains concentrated during financial crises and
earnings seasons.  During calm markets, the sentiment signal adds
negligible information because lagged $\RV$ already captures the
low-volatility regime.

\begin{warning}[NLP Gains Are Small and Fragile]
A 1--3\% $\QLIKE$ improvement is economically meaningful only if you
trade on volatility forecasts at scale.  The improvement is
sample-dependent, sensitive to the choice of news source, and does not
survive aggressive transaction costs in short-horizon
strategies~\citep{RahimikiaZohrenPoon2021NLP}.  Do not overfit to the
headline number.
\end{warning}

\citet{RahimikiaPoon2020} provide additional evidence that hybrid models
combining econometric baselines with text-derived features outperform
pure text-based approaches, reinforcing the ``let the baseline work
first'' principle of this chapter.

\section{Ensemble: HAR + LightGBM}\label{sec:hybrid:ensemble}

\subsection{The Safest Performer}

If you must choose one approach from this chapter for a production
volatility forecast, choose a weighted average of HAR and LightGBM.
It is simple, robust, and remarkably hard to beat.  Two combination
strategies dominate practice.

\subsubsection{Strategy A: Fixed Weighted Average}

Choose a fixed weight $w \in [0,1]$ and combine:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-avg}
  \hat{y}_{t+1} = w\,\widehat{\HAR}_t + (1-w)\,\widehat{\text{GBM}}_t,
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $w$ --- the HAR weight, typically calibrated on a validation set
        or set to $w = 0.7$ as a robust default,
  \item $\widehat{\HAR}_t$ --- the HAR forecast from
        Equation~\eqref{eq:har-fit-hybrid},
  \item $\widehat{\text{GBM}}_t$ --- the LightGBM forecast trained on the
        full feature set of Chapter~\ref{ch:features}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}[The 70/30 Rule]
A 70/30 HAR/LightGBM blend is remarkably hard to beat.  It inherits HAR's
stability in calm markets and LightGBM's ability to capture nonlinear
effects during crises.  You can optimize $w$ on validation data, but the
gain over a fixed 70/30 split is usually small.
\end{keyidea}

\subsubsection{Strategy B: Stacking with Ridge Meta-Learner}

Instead of fixing $w$, learn the combination weights via ridge regression
on out-of-sample predictions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Generate OOS predictions from HAR and LightGBM using
        time-series cross-validation (expanding or rolling window).
  \item Stack these predictions as features and fit a ridge regression:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:stacking}
          \hat{y}_{t+1} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1\,\widehat{\HAR}_t^{\text{OOS}}
            + \alpha_2\,\widehat{\text{GBM}}_t^{\text{OOS}},
        \end{equation}
        where:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item $\widehat{\HAR}_t^{\text{OOS}}$ and
                $\widehat{\text{GBM}}_t^{\text{OOS}}$ are out-of-sample
                predictions from the base models,
          \item $\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \alpha_2$ are learned by ridge
                regression with penalty $\lambda$ chosen by
                cross-validation,
          \item the ridge penalty prevents the meta-learner from
                overfitting to the small differences between base
                models.
        \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{workedexample}{Stacking on Five Test Days}

Suppose you have OOS predictions from HAR and LightGBM on 5 test days,
along with realized values:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
Day & $\widehat{\HAR}$ & $\widehat{\text{GBM}}$ & $\RV_{t+1}$ (actual) \\
\midrule
1 & 12.1 & 13.5 & 13.0 \\
2 & 18.7 & 19.2 & 20.1 \\
3 & 10.3 & 11.0 & 10.8 \\
4 & 25.4 & 22.8 & 23.5 \\
5 &  9.8 & 10.4 & 10.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

A ridge meta-learner fit on these data might yield weights
$\hat{\alpha}_1 = 0.45$, $\hat{\alpha}_2 = 0.58$ (they need not sum
to 1 when an intercept is included).  For day~4, the stacked forecast is
\[
  \hat{y}_5 = \hat{\alpha}_0 + 0.45 \times 25.4 + 0.58 \times 22.8
  = \hat{\alpha}_0 + 24.65.
\]
Compare the component MSEs: HAR alone achieves MSE $= 2.06$, LightGBM
alone achieves MSE $= 0.89$, and the stacked combination achieves
MSE $= 0.52$.  The ensemble reduces error beyond what either base model
achieves individually, illustrating the diversification benefit.

\end{workedexample}

\section{When to Use Pure ML vs.\ Hybrid}\label{sec:hybrid:decision}

The table below summarizes the practical decision you face when choosing
a forecasting architecture.  The default should be hybrid; deviate only
with good reason.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Architecture decision guide for volatility forecasting.}
\label{tab:decision-guide}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.5cm}p{3.2cm}p{3.0cm}p{3.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Architecture} & \textbf{Best When} & \textbf{Feature Set} &
  \textbf{Typical Setting} \\
\midrule
Pure HAR / HARQ
  & Only RV lags available; small sample ($<500$ days)
  & RV lags only
  & Quick benchmark; limited data access \\
\addlinespace
Hybrid (HAR + ML)
  & Rich features available; daily or weekly horizon; reliability matters
  & RV lags + engineered features
  & Production forecasts; research baseline \\
\addlinespace
Pure trees (GBM/XGB)
  & Rich features; intraday horizons; large sample ($>2000$ days)
  & Full feature set
  & High-frequency desks; feature importance studies \\
\addlinespace
Pure deep learning
  & Raw sequential data; cross-asset pooling; very large sample
  & Raw returns / order flow
  & Multi-asset platforms; representation learning \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{keyidea}[Default to Hybrid]
Your default architecture should be hybrid, not pure ML.  Start with HAR
as a backbone and add ML complexity only where the residuals justify it.
You earn the right to go pure ML only when you have a large sample, rich
features, and evidence that the linear baseline leaves substantial
structure in the residuals~\citep{RahimikiaPoon2020}.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Practical Checklist}

Before committing to an architecture, answer these questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{How large is your sample?}  Below 500 daily observations,
        pure HAR is hard to beat.  ML needs data.
  \item \textbf{Do you have features beyond RV lags?}  If not, a hybrid
        adds nothing because the ML stage has no new information.
  \item \textbf{What is your forecast horizon?}  At weekly or monthly
        horizons, HAR's multi-scale structure dominates.  ML gains
        concentrate at the daily horizon.
  \item \textbf{How much do you value interpretability?}  HAR coefficients
        are directly interpretable.  A hybrid with SHAP on the ML
        component preserves partial interpretability.  Pure deep learning
        sacrifices it.
  \item \textbf{What is your retraining budget?}  Hybrids are cheap:
        refit HAR monthly, retrain ML weekly.  Pure deep learning demands
        more compute.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary}\label{sec:hybrid:summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Hybrid models decompose the forecasting problem into a linear
        component (HAR, GARCH) and a nonlinear residual component (ML),
        allowing each method to operate where it is strongest.
  \item HAR explains 40--60\% of next-day RV with three coefficients;
        training ML on the residual rather than the raw target improves
        signal-to-noise and prevents the ML model from wasting capacity
        on structure that a linear model already captures.
  \item HAR-SVR uses support vector regression with an
        $\varepsilon$-insensitive loss on HAR residuals, automatically
        ignoring days where HAR is ``close enough'' and focusing on the
        hard cases.
  \item GINN hard-wires the GARCH recursion into a neural network, letting
        the network learn time-varying corrections to GARCH parameters
        rather than learning volatility dynamics from
        scratch~\citep{GINN2024}.
  \item NLP-augmented HAR (Word2Vec sentiment) yields 1--3\% $\QLIKE$
        improvement concentrated in crisis periods, but gains are fragile
        and source-dependent~\citep{RahimikiaZohrenPoon2021NLP}.
  \item A simple 70/30 HAR/LightGBM weighted average is the safest
        production choice and is remarkably hard to beat with more
        complex ensembles.
  \item Stacking via a ridge meta-learner on OOS predictions adapts the
        combination weights to the data while guarding against overfitting
        at the meta-level.
  \item Pure ML (trees or deep learning) earns its place only when you
        have large samples, rich features, and evidence that the linear
        baseline leaves exploitable structure in residuals.
  \item Your default architecture should be hybrid: let HAR do the heavy
        lifting, then train ML on what remains.
  \item When evaluating hybrids, always report the base model's
        standalone performance alongside the hybrid, so the reader can
        judge the marginal contribution of the ML component.
  \item Ensemble diversification provides error reduction even when the ML
        component is only modestly accurate, as long as its errors are
        weakly correlated with the base model's errors.
  \item All combination weights and meta-learner parameters must be tuned
        on out-of-sample data to avoid inflating the apparent benefit of
        the hybrid.
\end{itemize}
